Here begins the book's second half, **Part II: How LLMs Are Put to Work**.
In Part I we toured the model's **insides**.
Turning words into numbers, measuring closeness, learning from mistakes, generating one word at a time.
In Part II we look at how you actually **use** that clever box to build things.

## The screen everyone pictures

When you hear "use an AI," the thing you picture is probably that screen.
You type a question into a box, and the reply appears below — a **chat screen** like ChatGPT.
Ask in words, get an answer in words.
So let's start with "that screen" itself — here it is.
Send a question and watch the reply come out.

<ChatScreen lang="en" client:visible />

It's very convenient.
In fact, for most people in the world, "using an AI" simply **means chatting on this screen**.
Looking things up, getting help with writing — chat covers it.
You never need to build an app, and nothing is missing.

## Then why talk about "building" at all?

And yet this Part II is about to talk about **using the model from a program**.
If chat is enough, why?

The reason appears the moment you look around you.
The weather app on your phone, auto-captions on videos, the camera that translates a foreign menu, the "questions? chat here" box on a shopping site.
Behind features like these, the same LLMs are — more and more — working as **components inside apps**.
And nobody there is opening a chat screen.
**A program inside the app is talking to the model in a person's place.**

So "how to use AI" has two faces.
The first: you, chatting on a screen, directly.
The second: a program calling the model and putting it to work behind a feature.
Part II stars the second one.
Even if you never plan to build an app, knowing this underside makes "AI-powered" features far less mysterious — why they behave the way they do, and how far to trust them.

So what does "a program calls the model" actually mean?

## A model is a window you can call

The clue is the chat screen you just used.
When you pressed that send button, what happened behind it?
A request was sent across the network to the model on the other side, and a reply came back.
The model is not sitting inside your computer or phone.
Usually it lives **across the network**, and screens and programs **call** it to use it.

The shape of the exchange is the same as ordinary web use.
Just as a browser asks a site "show me this page" and gets the contents back, a program sends the model a **request** ("answer this") and the model sends back a **response**.
The side that asks is the **client**; the side that answers is the **server**.

<RequestResponse lang="en" />

So think of a model as "a window that offers a text-returning feature, callable across the network."
It's in the same family as search, translation, or maps, which you call across the network the same way.
The chat screen itself is just one of many apps calling this window behind the scenes.

## Change the reader, and everything changes

Here is the "why?" that runs through all of Part II.
On the chat screen, the thing reading the model's reply was a **human**.
Humans are clever, so a little vagueness is fine — we fill it in from context.
If the reply is "Tokyo will be sunny tomorrow, up to around 28°C," we take the meaning without any effort.

But inside an app, the thing receiving that reply is a **program**.
And a program cannot read the way a human does.
It doesn't understand what it reads — it just processes sequences of characters, mechanically, by fixed steps.
Reliably pulling just "28" out of "up to around 28°C," every single time, is actually very hard.
Today it's "28°C," but tomorrow it might be "roughly 30 degrees," and the day after, "a bit chilly, isn't it?"
A human reads all of those; to a program they're differently-shaped, awkward data every time.

## The problem Part II solves

This is where Part II begins.

> **The model returns free-form text. But the program receiving it is worst at exactly that — free-form text.**

How do we bridge that mismatch?
Bridge it, and the model's reply can flow into the next step of processing.
Once it flows, the model stops being a "chat partner" and becomes a **component in an app**.
And once it's a component, what it can do opens up fast — using tools, taking on multi-step jobs.

Before that first step, let's look a little closer at what rides inside the request we send to the window.
Besides the question text, a request can carry settings that change how the reply comes out.
