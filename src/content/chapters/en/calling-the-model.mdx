## The screen everyone pictures

Say "using AI", and what comes to mind is probably that screen —
the conversation view, like ChatGPT's, where you type a question into a box and the reply appears below.
We've put "that screen" right here.
Send a question and watch the reply come out.

<ChatScreen lang="en" client:visible />

It is very convenient.
For most people in the world, "using AI" simply is having a conversation on this screen.
It covers looking things up and drafting text alike; nobody needs to build an app to get value.

## Then why talk about building at all?

And yet this Part II is about to discuss using the model from a program.
If chat suffices, why bother?

Look around you and the reason appears.
The weather app on your phone, auto-captions on videos, the camera that translates a foreign menu when you point it, the "questions here" chat on a shopping site.
Behind features like these, the same LLMs are increasingly at work as components of apps.
Nobody there has a conversation screen open.
A program inside the app is exchanging messages with the model on a person's behalf.

So "using AI" has two faces.
One: you converse directly on a screen.
Two: a program calls the model and puts it to work behind a feature.
Part II is about the second.
Even if you never plan to build an app, knowing this backstage view shows you why "AI-powered" features behave the way they do — and how far to trust them.

So what does "a program calls the model" actually mean?

## The model is a counter you can call

The clue is in the chat screen itself.
What happens behind the scenes when you press send?
A request travels over the network to the model, and a reply travels back.
The model is not inside your computer or phone.
It usually lives across the network, and screens and programs call it.

The shape of the exchange is the same as the everyday web.
Just as your browser asks a site "show me this page" and gets the content back, a program sends the model a **request** ("answer this") and the model sends back a **response**.
The asking side is called the **client**; the answering side, the **server**.

<RequestResponse lang="en" />

Think of the model as a counter that offers "text in, text out" in a form callable over the network.
Search, translation, and maps are callable over the network the same way; the model is one of the family.
The chat screen, too, is just one of many apps quietly calling this counter behind the curtain.

## A different reader, different needs

Here the question that runs through all of Part II appears.
On the conversation screen, the reader of the model's reply was a human.
Humans are clever; even a somewhat vague sentence gets understood from context.
"Tokyo will be sunny tomorrow, going up to around 28°C" — we absorb that effortlessly.

Inside an app, though, the reply is received by a program.
And a program does not read the way a human does.
It doesn't understand sentences; it processes strings of characters mechanically, by predetermined steps.
Reliably extracting just the "28" from "going up to around 28°C", every single time, is surprisingly hard.
Today it says "around 28°C"; tomorrow, "roughly 30 degrees or so"; the day after, "a bit chilly out there."
A human reads all of these; to a program, it is data whose shape changes every time.

## The problem Part II solves

This is Part II's starting point:

> **The model returns free-form text. But the program receiving it is worst at handling exactly that.**

How do you close this gap?
Close it, and the model's replies can feed into the next step of processing.
Feed them onward, and the model turns from a conversation partner into an app component.
Become a component, and the possibilities open wide: handing it tools, trusting it with multi-step work.

Before that first step, one thing is worth pinning down.
The request going to the counter — what is in it, besides the question?
