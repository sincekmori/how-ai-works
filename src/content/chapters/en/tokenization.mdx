Suppose we hand the sentence "I really love cats" to a machine.
Before it can do anything else, the machine has to decide where to cut the text.
This cutting job is called **tokenization**, and each piece it produces is called a **token**.

Having a name for the job doesn't solve it.
Where should the cuts go?

## The parts list has to be fixed in advance

Before comparing cutting schemes, one thing is worth pinning down.
The previous chapter said there are tens to hundreds of thousands of candidates for the next word.
Every round, the model picks one word from that candidate list.
That candidate list is decided when the model is built, and then it is frozen.
The model can only read and write pieces that appear on the list.
This list is called the **vocabulary**.

So "where do we cut?" is really another question in disguise: "what pieces should go on a finite list?"

## What happens if we cut by words

The most natural idea is to cut at word boundaries.
But the world's supply of words never stops growing.
New slang, typos, emoji, technical jargon — there is no end to what the list would need to hold.
We want the vocabulary to be finite, yet no size is ever enough.
Worse, the moment a word outside the list shows up, the model simply cannot read that part.

## What happens if we cut into single characters

Then how about the opposite — one character per piece?
The number of characters is limited, so the list will never overflow.

A different problem appears instead.
The model writes one piece per prediction, so the finer the pieces, the more rounds it takes to produce the same sentence.
Running hundreds of predictions for a short reply is wasteful.
And a single character carries almost no meaning on its own.
Seeing just the letter "c" tells you nothing about whether the word is "cat" or "cup".

## Making pieces out of frequent chunks

Cutting by words breaks the list; cutting by characters makes the sequence too long.
So we take the middle ground.
Chunks that appear often become single pieces, and rare words are spelled out as combinations of smaller pieces.
This is the mainstream approach today.

With this scheme, common words are written short, as one piece each.
Rare words can always be assembled from smaller pieces, so "stuck on an unknown word" never happens.
And the vocabulary stays at a manageable size.

## How the pieces are chosen: BPE

Who decides which chunks count as "frequent", and how?
The classic method is **BPE** (Byte Pair Encoding).
It has exactly one move:
take the two pieces that sit next to each other most often, and glue them into one — then repeat.

Suppose the word "low" appears many times in your text.
With everything split into single characters, the sequence "l" followed by "o" shows up again and again.
So you glue "l" and "o" into a new piece, "lo", and add it to the vocabulary.
Then you glue the next most frequent pair, and the next.
This continues until the vocabulary reaches the size you want.

The reason to glue frequent pairs first is that it lets sentences be written as shorter strings of pieces.
The more common a chunk, the bigger the saving from bundling it into one piece.

## Cut into words before counting

The "most frequent neighbouring pair" is not counted across the whole text as one long string.
If it were, the end of one word and the start of the next would count as a pair too.
In English text, a word-final "s" followed by a space occurs constantly, so a meaningless "s + space" piece would be glued almost immediately.
So the text is first cut into words at spaces and punctuation, and the gluing happens only inside a word.
Nothing is ever glued across a word boundary.
Languages without spaces, such as Japanese, need a different arrangement.
Some schemes treat a run of characters up to the next punctuation mark as one chunk, and others skip this step and process the text as it is.

The same word shows up thousands or millions of times, so counting it afresh at every occurrence would be wasteful.
Instead, each distinct word is listed once with the number of times it appears, and every pair inside it is weighted by that number.
The "×4" and "×3" at the left of the demo below are those counts.
If "low" appears 4 times, the pair "l + o" counts 4 from that word alone.

## What BPE leaves behind: a list of pieces, and an order

Once training is over, how does new text get split?
BPE leaves two things behind.
One is the finished list of pieces, the vocabulary.
The other is an **ordered list of merge rules**: which pair was glued first, which second, and so on.
To split new text, you start from single characters and replay the rules in exactly the order they were learned.
The "Merge rules learned" in the demo is that list, and the lower part of the demo shows it being replayed on a new word.

To see why the order matters, take the word "slower", which is not in the demo's text.
After rules 1 and 2 it reads "s | low | e | r", and two rules now compete for "low": rule 3 glues "s + low", and rule 5 glues "low + e".
With two candidates, something has to decide.
That is what the order is for.
Rule 3 comes first, so the word becomes "slow | e | r", and rule 5 no longer finds a "low" to work on.
Had rule 5 gone first, the word would have become "s | lower" instead.
Because the order is fixed, every piece of text splits exactly one way.

<MergeOrder lang="en" />

This "same answer every time" matters more than it looks.
The model learned language from text split in one particular way.
If the split drifted at the time of use, the model would be looking at sequences of pieces it never saw, and what it learned would not carry over.

One more thing to notice: pick "cat" in the lower part of the demo, and the pieces "c" and "a" get a dashed outline.
A character that is not among the starting pieces matches no rule at all, and the model cannot read it.
Is a character outside the list a dead end?

## What about Japanese, accents, and emoji?

The demo used the letters that appeared in its text as the starting pieces.
For English, letters, digits and punctuation fit in about a hundred pieces.
Many languages are not so compact.
Japanese has two syllabaries plus kanji: the everyday set alone has 2,136 characters, and with names and older words the count runs into the tens of thousands.
Such a large starting set bloats the vocabulary, and worse still is any character that never appeared in the training text.
Like "c" and "a" above, a character missing from the list cannot be built from anything, and the model has to skip it.

The way out is to start from something finer than a character, and shared by the whole world.
A computer stores every character as a run of numbers from 0 to 255.
Each of those numbers is called a **byte**.
One worldwide rule, UTF-8, fixes which numbers each character becomes: the letter "a" is the single byte 97, and the Japanese character "ね" is the three bytes 227, 129, 173.

<CharBytes lang="en" />

So instead of characters, the starting pieces are the 256 possible byte values.
The text is split into bytes rather than characters, and then the very same BPE runs on top.
The starting set is 256 pieces whatever the language, and every character in every script, emoji included, is some combination of those 256.
An "unreadable character" cannot happen, in principle.
Most of today's models use this **byte-level BPE**.

The gluing goes exactly as before.
Every hiragana starts with the bytes 227, 129 (or 227, 130).
In Japanese text, the pair "227 + 129" is by far the most common neighbouring pair, so it is glued very early.
Next, that "227 129" glued with 173 gives "ね", glued with 147 gives "こ", and then "ね + こ" gives "ねこ".
Bytes build characters, and characters grow into chunks of words.

There is one side effect.
The gluing ignores character boundaries, so a piece can end mid-character: say, the last two bytes of one character plus the first byte of the next.
When the model writes such a piece, the software that shows the text waits for the next piece until the character is complete before displaying it.

A different scheme also exists: keep whole characters as the starting pieces, and fall back to bytes only for a rare character that is missing from the list.
Only the starting point differs; the procedure of gluing the most frequent neighbours is the same either way.

## Why some languages cost more tokens

The same message often takes more tokens in Japanese, or in many other languages, than in English.
The reasons are already on this page.

The first is the starting length.
An English letter is 1 byte and a Japanese character is almost always 3, so before any gluing, Japanese starts out three times as long.
The second is how far the gluing gets.
BPE glues the most frequent pairs first, so if most of the training text is English, English words quickly become single pieces.
Japanese sequences rarely get their turn and tend to remain as single characters, or even as fragments of a character.
The 256 starting pieces are fair to every language, but the finished vocabulary mirrors the language mix of the text it was trained on.

More tokens means more cost and more time.
Processing effort, price, and how much text fits in one reading are all measured in tokens.
Recent models have grown their vocabularies to between one and two hundred thousand pieces, and balance their training text across languages, partly to narrow this gap.

## Good things to know

- **What is on the list**: with byte-level BPE, the vocabulary holds the 256 starting bytes, the pieces created by gluing, and a few dedicated pieces (special tokens) that mark where text begins and ends or which speaker a message belongs to. GPT-2's vocabulary, for example, is 256 + 50,000 glued pieces + 1 special token = 50,257 entries.
- **Every model splits differently**: the list and the rules are fixed when a model is built, so the same sentence splits differently, into a different number of tokens, from one model to the next.

This "cut into frequent chunks" entrance is shared by today's major models — GPT, Llama, Claude, Gemini, Mistral, Qwen, and more.

Text is now a sequence of tokens.
But each token is still made of letters, like "cat".
How is a machine that can only do arithmetic supposed to handle a piece made of letters?
