Agents can now run their loop and finish multi-step work.
Use one every day, though, and a new frustration surfaces.
It is smart — and it does not know how you do things.

## Capable, but ignorant of "the way we do it here"

Say your family makes a trip booklet before every vacation.
Your family's booklet has a fixed form:
destination and dates on the cover, a schedule table per day, a packing list at the end.
Ask the agent, and it will happily make a booklet.
But say nothing more, and what comes out is a generic booklet, shaped however the agent pleases.
So you explain — "cover like this, schedule as a table, packing list last" — and get exactly the booklet you wanted.

The problem is the next trip.
Start a new conversation, and the agent remembers none of your explanation.
The model reads only the text handed to it within that conversation.
No memory survives outside it.
So you explain again, from the top.
Every trip, every time.
The agent is, in effect, a highly capable new hire:
handles almost anything competently, but "the way we do it here" stays unknown until taught.

## Fine — hand it everything?

Write one manual with all your ways in it, and hand it over at the start of every conversation.
Tempting — and while the procedures are few, it genuinely works.

But the things you want to teach keep growing.
The booklet format, the class-newsletter layout, the receipt-reimbursement rules, the photo-organizing scheme.
What happens when the manual hits fifty pages?

Recall that a model can only read so much at once.
The size of the desk where handed-over text gets spread out is called the **context window**.
When a model's spec page says "context window: 200K tokens", that is this desk's size.
The tokenization chapter's "how much can be read at once is measured in tokens", and the frontier chapter's long-context race — both were about this desk.
Desks grow year by year, yet their surface stays finite.

And bigger is not automatically safer.
The model reads everything on the desk, every time.
The more you pile on, the slower the reply and the higher the bill (the time-and-money problem from the agents chapter).
Besides — nobody spreads every cookbook in the house across the desk to make one curry tonight.
Workspace shrinks, and a mountain of irrelevant procedures in view invites confusion of its own.
Keep the desk to what the current job needs.
That is this chapter's starting point.

## On the shelf, spines out

One trick answers it: bundle each procedure into its own playbook, and line them up on a bookshelf.
Day to day, the agent sees only the spines —
each playbook's name and one-line description.

When a job arrives, the agent scans the spines first.
Asked "make the trip booklet", the "trip booklet" spine catches its eye.
It takes that one volume off the shelf, opens it, and follows the steps.
The rest stay shelved.
On the desk, always: a few lines of spines, plus the single opened volume.
The remaining space stays free for the work itself.
This arrangement — spines by default, open only the one that fits — is **Agent Skills**.
Each playbook on the shelf is a **skill**.

## A skill is just a folder

What a skill actually is turns out to be surprisingly plain.
One skill is one folder.
At its center sits a single text file named SKILL.md.
Near the top: a name and a one-line description — the spine.
Below that: the steps.
Written the way you would teach a person.
"First, destination and dates on the cover. Then each day's schedule as a table. Packing list last."

The folder holds more than SKILL.md, though.
A booklet template, reference material like a phrase collection, small programs for fixed calculations or conversions.
Anything meant to be used together goes in the same folder, and the text can point at it: "use this."

The reading happens in a fixed order.

- The spine (name and description) is always on the desk.
- The body (the steps) is opened only when the job fits.
- Templates and materials are read only at the moment the steps call for them.
- The small programs are not read at all: rather than spreading their contents on the desk, the agent runs them like tools and takes back only the result.

Hundreds of lines of program in a skill barely smudge the desk.

There is not a single piece of new magic here.
The model reads the text it is handed and follows it — as this book has shown all along.
A skill is a filing system: a way to portion out the text you hand over, and store it well.

A comparison with Part I is worth making.
Fine-tuning was the way to teach a model something — collect examples, change the weights, retrain.
Skills are a different, far lighter road.

- No training; it works the moment it is written.
- Got something wrong? Rewrite the text, and it's fixed.
- No programming knowledge needed; anyone who can write can make one.
- Hand the folder over, and your family's or colleagues' agents work the same way.

## Peeking at a real one

To keep this from staying abstract, look at one real skill.
Anthropic (the maker of Claude) publishes the skills it actually uses [on GitHub](https://github.com/anthropics/skills).
Among them, the [pdf skill](https://github.com/anthropics/skills/tree/main/skills/pdf) is the playbook for PDF paperwork — reading, merging, filling in forms.
Here is what the folder holds:

```text
pdf/
├── SKILL.md        ← the playbook itself
├── forms.md        ← material: detailed steps for filling forms
├── reference.md    ← material: a catalog of advanced operations
└── scripts/        ← the small programs
    ├── fill_fillable_fields.py   (fill in a form)
    ├── extract_form_field_info.py (inspect the fields)
    └── …
```

And here is how [the real SKILL.md](https://github.com/anthropics/skills/blob/main/skills/pdf/SKILL.md) opens (abridged):

```text
---
name: pdf
description: Use this skill whenever the user wants to do anything with PDF files. This includes reading or extracting text/tables, merging PDFs, splitting, rotating pages, adding watermarks, creating new PDFs, filling PDF forms, and more. If the user mentions a .pdf file or asks to produce one, use this skill.
---

# PDF Processing Guide

This guide covers essential PDF processing operations. For advanced features, see reference.md. If you need to fill out a PDF form, read forms.md and follow its instructions.

(the concrete steps follow…)
```

Three things to notice.
First, the name and description at the top: that is the spine, introducing itself — "PDF job? Open me."
Second, the body points at material in the same folder — "see reference.md", "read forms.md and follow it."
Only when a form-filling job arrives does forms.md land on the desk.
The reading order from earlier is realized in exactly this way.
Third, the small programs in scripts/.
Fixed work like filling in the form is run, not read — the agent takes back only the result.

So when you ask "fill in this PDF application form", the agent picks the pdf skill by its spine, opens SKILL.md, follows it to forms.md, and runs the scripts to do the filling.
Everything this chapter has described happens inside that one folder.

## If tools are hands, skills are playbooks

How do skills differ from the tools and MCP of the previous chapters?
The roles differ.
Tools extend what an agent can do:
search, calculate, send email.
Hands, in a word.
Skills teach an agent how to do things:
in what order, in what form, minding what.
Playbooks, in a word.
No number of weather tools will ever produce "our family's booklet format".
And a playbook alone moves nothing without tools to look things up.
Hands and playbooks — both together make a worker you can rely on.

## Where can they be used?

By now you may be wondering whether you can start today.
The answer splits between writing and using.

Writing: anyone, starting today.
A skill is just a folder and a text file; no special equipment.

Using takes support on the agent's side.
First, the agent must be able to read folders —
gather the spines into a shelf, open the volume that fits: that motion has to exist.
For steps-only skills, that is enough.
At the extreme, pasting SKILL.md's contents by hand into an unsupporting chat still conveys the meaning.
The skills mechanism is what does that automatically, in an organized way.

Skills that include small programs add one more requirement:
the agent must have a workspace where programs can run.
Most supporting apps provide that workspace inside an enclosure called a **sandbox**.
Like a playground sandbox: inside the fence you may dig and build freely, but the outside — your whole computer, the wider network — stays out of casual reach.
The fence exists so that when a program is wrong (or malicious), the damage stops inside the sand.

So skills are not "any screen, any time".
They are used inside apps and agent environments that support them —
support that is spreading through chat apps' document features and developer-facing agent tools.

## Cautions

A skill is precisely the instructions an agent follows.
If the playbook is wrong, the agent will be wrong — confidently.
There are also places that distribute skills other people wrote.
Convenient — but just as you wouldn't cook an important dinner from a stranger's unread recipe, check the contents before use.
The small programs in a skill folder get executed, like tools.
"There's a sandbox, so it's fine" is not a full answer:
even inside the fence, the files you handed the agent and the tools you connected are within reach.
The agents chapter's "irreversible move" and its safety devices apply to skills unchanged.

## Becoming a common shape

The MCP chapter argued that common agreements pay off for everyone.
The same is happening with skills.
Agent Skills began as one company's idea (Anthropic's), but the folder shape and writing format are a published, common format, and supporting models and apps are spreading.
Since a skill is just a folder, it travels well.
Write a playbook once, reuse it across many agents.
As structured output standardized the shape of replies and MCP the wiring of tools, skills are standardizing the teaching of procedures.

With that, every part this book set out to build is in place.
Know the insides, call from outside, receive in fixed shapes, hand over tools, run the loop, teach the procedures.
All that remains is to close the journey.
