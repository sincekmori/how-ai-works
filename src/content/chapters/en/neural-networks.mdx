The machine that computes the allocation table is a **neural network**:
a contraption made of vast numbers of simple calculations stacked together, covered in adjustable "knobs".
It is the inside of the thing we have been calling "the model".
This chapter looks at how it computes, and how it learns.

## The smallest part: the neuron

A network is a collection of small parts called **neurons**.
Here is everything one neuron does.

<Neuron lang="en" />

Multiply each incoming number by its own **weight**, then add everything up.
For example, with inputs (0.5, 0.9, 0.2) and weights (0.8, −0.5, 0.3):

0.5 × 0.8 + 0.9 × (−0.5) + 0.2 × 0.3 = 0.4 − 0.45 + 0.06 = 0.01

Finally, the result passes through a **switch** (the activation).

What are the weights for?
They express how much attention to pay to each input.
A large weight means important; a negative weight means it works in reverse.
The model's entire character is decided by the values of these weights (the "parameter count" you'll meet in later chapters is simply how many weights there are — billions and more, for an LLM).

And what is the switch for?
Without it — with additions alone — any number of stacked layers would collapse into one big addition.
The switch adds a bend, a "react strongly only under this condition", and that bend is what makes complex behavior expressible at all.

## Side by side into layers, stacked to go deep

A row of neurons side by side makes a **layer**.
Layers stacked several deep make a neural network.

<NeuralNetFlow lang="en" />

The point of stacking is that each layer can assemble more complex clues out of the clues the previous layer found.
For text: the first layer picks up features of words, the next layer turns of phrase, the next the meaning of the whole sentence — building from simple to complex, stage by stage.

Computation flows from input to output, and a prediction comes out.
This forward flow is called the **forward pass**.

## Why make it learn at all?

A freshly built network has random weights, so its predictions are way off.
We are about to fix the weights — but first, one natural question deserves an answer.
Wouldn't it be more reliable for humans to write down all the rules of language and hand them over?

Researchers tried exactly that.
And they got stuck.
Language is a pile of exceptions.
Take "a dog" and "the dog".
You choose between them correctly without thinking, yet nobody — not even the experts — can state the rule behind the choice so that it covers every case.
What we cannot articulate, we cannot write down as rules.
The lesson left standing after decades of effort was this:
stop writing rules, show the machine mountains of text, and let the machine tune itself to match.

"Tuning" is a three-step loop.
Make a prediction.
Compare it with the correct answer, using the previous chapter's shares to measure the size of the miss.
Turn the weights a little, in the direction that shrinks the miss.

The trouble is that last step.
There are millions of weights.
Which way, and by how much, should each one turn?
Couldn't we turn them at random and keep whatever helps?
In principle that inches forward, but with this many knobs, luck alone would outlast the universe.
What randomness lacks is directional information —
a clue that says "turn this knob this way, and the miss will shrink."

## Finding the downhill: the gradient

That clue comes from how things change.
Mathematics calls it a slope, and the slopes of all the weights taken together are called the **gradient**.

Picture climbing down a mountain in thick fog.
You cannot see far at all, but by feeling the ground at your feet, you can tell one thing: which direction is steepest downhill.
Take one step that way.
Feel the ground again; take another step.
Repeat, and eventually you reach the valley floor — the place where the miss is smallest.
That is what learning is.

<GradientDescent lang="en" />

Written as a phrase, one step of learning is just this:

**new value = current value − (step size) × (slope)**

The minus sign at the front means "move against the direction that increases the miss".
The steeper the slope — the more that weight matters to the miss — the bigger the move.

Why single steps, though?
Why not one giant stride?
Because in fog, the only slope you know is the one under your feet.
A slope is local information; it says nothing about terrain farther away.
A giant stride might vault over the valley floor and land you on the opposite face.
So: a little at a time, as many times as it takes.
In LLM training, this step is repeated billions of times.

## Handing mistakes backwards: backpropagation

One problem remains: how do you get the slope for millions of weights at once?
Testing each weight by nudging it would take forever.

There is a clever trick.
Hand the mistake backwards, from output toward input.
The gap at the output is assigned, one stage at a time, to the weights before it — in the form "how much of the blame for that gap belongs to this weight in this layer?"
This is called **backpropagation** (the upward orange arrows in the figure above).

The reason to go backwards is that the size of the mistake is only known at one place: the final output.
Passing the blame back from there, stage by stage, computes "how to fix it" for every weight in a single backward sweep.

At the core of the blame-passing sits a rule called the **chain rule**:
influence gets multiplied as it passes through each stage.
If the first stage amplifies by 2 and the next by 3, the total effect is 2 × 3 = 6.
So multiplying each stage's factor backwards tells you exactly how much a deep weight affects the final mistake.
