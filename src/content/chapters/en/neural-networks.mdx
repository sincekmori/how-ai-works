At the end of the previous chapter we said that once a miss has a size, a bridge to learning opens up.
In this chapter we cross that bridge.
First, the stage where learning happens: the "model" itself.
It is a **neural network** — a contraption built from huge piles of simple arithmetic, with lots of "knobs."
In this chapter we will see **how it computes and how it learns**.

## The smallest part: the neuron

A network is a collection of small parts called **neurons**.
What a single neuron does is just this much.

<Neuron lang="en" />

Each incoming number is multiplied by its own **weight**, and the results are added up.
For example, with inputs (0.5, 0.9, 0.2) and weights (0.8, −0.5, 0.3):

0.5 × 0.8 ＋ 0.9 × (−0.5) ＋ 0.2 × 0.3 ＝ 0.4 − 0.45 ＋ 0.06 ＝ **0.01**

Then the result is passed through a **switch** (an activation).
Here, hold on to two "whys."
**Why do we need weights?**
To express "which input matters, and how much" (a big weight means important; a negative weight means it pushes the other way).
**Why do we need the switch?**
Because if there were no switch and we only added things up, then no matter how many layers we stack, it all collapses back into a single addition and can't express anything complicated.
By adding a bend — like "react strongly only under certain conditions" — the switch is what finally gives the network rich expressive power.

## Line them up for a "layer," stack them to go "deep"

A bunch of neurons placed side by side makes one **layer**.
Stack those layers many levels high and you have a neural network.

<NeuralNetFlow lang="en" />

**Why stack them (go deep)?**
Because the next layer can use the clues the previous layer found as raw material to build up more complex clues.
For text, for instance, the first layer might catch "features of individual words," the next "turns of phrase," the next "the meaning of the whole sentence" — building up step by step, from the simple to the complex.

Computation flows from input to output and an answer (a prediction) comes out — this forward direction of flow is called the **forward pass**.

## How does it learn?

In a freshly made network the "knobs" (the weights) are random, so the predictions are way off.
Before fixing that, let's start one "why?" deeper.
**Why make the machine "learn" at all?
Wouldn't it be more reliable for humans to write down all the rules of language and hand them over?**

Researchers actually tried exactly that.
And, after decades, they got stuck.
Language is a **pile of exceptions**.
Take "a dog" and "the dog": you use them correctly without thinking, but nobody — not even the experts — can write down a rule for the choice that covers every case without exceptions.
What we can't articulate, we can't write down as rules.
The hard-won lesson was this: "**instead of humans writing the rules, show the machine mountains of text and let it tune itself to match.**"

So what does "tune" mean?
The recipe is a three-beat loop.
**Make it guess → compare with the right answer and, using the shares from the previous chapter, measure the size of the miss → turn the knobs a little in the direction that shrinks the miss.**

The problem: there are **millions** of knobs.
Which ones, which way, and by how much?
How about turning them at random, and keeping whatever helps?
In principle that does inch forward.
But with this many knobs, luck alone wouldn't finish before the universe ends.
What randomness lacks is **direction**: a hint that says "turn this knob this way, and the miss shrinks."

## Finding the way downhill: the gradient

That hint comes from the **way things change**.
In math this is called the "slope", and the slopes of all the knobs together are called the **gradient**.
Picture coming down a mountain in thick fog.
You can't see far at all, but by checking the slope under your feet you can always tell "the steepest downhill direction".
Take one step that way.
Check again, step again.
Repeat, and eventually you reach the bottom of the valley — the place where the misses are smallest.
This is what learning really is.

<GradientDescent lang="en" />

Put into a word-formula, one step of learning is just this:

**new value = current value − (step size) × (slope)**

The "− (minus)" out front is the sign for "move against the increasing direction — move toward less."
The steeper the slope (= the more this knob affects the miss), the bigger the move.
**But why small steps?
Why not one giant leap?**
Because in fog, you only know the slope right under your feet.
A slope is strictly local information; it tells you nothing about the terrain far away.
Leap too far and you might sail clear over the valley and land on the far hillside.
So: a little at a time, as many times as it takes.
In LLM training, this one step is repeated trillions of times.

## Hand the mistake backward: backpropagation

One problem remains: how do we find this "slope" for **millions of knobs at once**?
Trying them one at a time, the day would be over before we finished.
There is a clever trick: **hand the mistake backward, from the output toward the input**.
The "gap" at the output is assigned, one layer at a time, toward the front, in the form of "how much is this knob in this layer responsible for that gap?"
This is called **backpropagation** (it's the upward orange arrow in the figure earlier).

Why does going "backward" work?
Because the place where we can see the size of the mistake is the very last output.
If we pass that responsibility backward, starting from the layers near the output and working toward the front, then for every single knob we get "how it should be fixed" — all in **one single sweep back**.

At the heart of how it hands the blame around is a rule called the **chain rule**.
"An influence gets multiplied by some factor every time it passes through a layer" — if it counts double at the first stage and triple at the next, then overall it's 2 × 3 ＝ 6 times.
So by multiplying together each stage's "by what factor" in the backward direction, we find out how much a deep knob affects the final mistake.

## Summary

- A **neuron** is a part that multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up, and passes the result through a switch (activation). The weights are "how much to value each input," the switch is "expressive power."
- Line up neurons into a layer, stack the layers, and you get a **neural network**. The deeper it is, the more it can build complex clues up from simple ones.
- Learning is the repeated loop of "predict with the **forward pass** → measure the size of the miss → use **backpropagation** to find each knob's slope, and step a little against the **gradient**." This is the foundation of every LLM.
