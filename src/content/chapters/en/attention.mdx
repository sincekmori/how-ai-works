Recall the wish we ended the previous chapter with.
Let the word currently being read go look directly at whichever words it relates to, near or far.
And let every word do it at once.
The mechanism that realizes this wish, exactly as stated, is **attention**.
True to its name, it decides which words get how much attention — and it becomes the heart of the Transformer in the chapter after this, shared by every major LLM today.

## One idea: read with weights

Think about reading a sentence like this:

"The dog threw the ball, so it chased **it**."

You knew instantly what that second "it" refers to.
The ball.
Your eyes drifted back, on their own, to "ball" earlier in the sentence.
Inside your head, the words were not all given equal weight — the strongly related ones got noticed strongly.

Attention has the machine do exactly this: read more strongly where the relation is stronger.
And not just for one word — it computes where to look for every word in the sentence at the same time.

## Three roles: query, key, value

How does a machine judge whether a relation is strong or weak?
For that, every word is given three roles.

<QueryKeyValue lang="en" />

A library makes a good picture.
The topic you are hunting for is the **query**.
The label on each book's spine is the **key**.
The book's contents are the **value**.
You compare your topic against every label on the shelf, and the better a label matches, the more closely you read that book's contents.
Attention does exactly this, between words.

## Score it, then turn it into shares

How is "how well the query and key match" measured?
This is the moment for the tool we built in the closeness-of-meaning chapter: the **dot product**.
The dot product, remember, awards points to aspects that agree and deducts points from aspects that clash, then totals everything into one number.
Take the dot product of a query and a key, and that value is the match score.
A good match scores large; an unrelated pair scores small.

Once the scores are in, they get converted into shares of attention.
Say the word "it" checks its query against every word's key, with this result:

<AttentionScores lang="en" />

The reason to convert rather than use raw scores is the same one from the probability chapter: we want an allocation that sums to 100%.
The conversion is called **softmax**.
Softmax gives bigger shares to higher scores, and it sharpens the differences while doing so, making the best match stand out clearly.
This is how a split like "84% on ball, 11% on dog, 4% on threw" gets decided.

Finally, the values get blended using those shares:
84% of ball's contents, 11% of dog's contents, and so on.
The result — information mixed in proportion to relevance — becomes the word's new contents.
That is one full round of attention.
The embeddings chapter said a word's meaning gets rebuilt to fit its context; this blending is the very operation that does the rebuilding.

## Self-attention: everyone looks at everyone, at once

So far we took one word's point of view.
In reality, every word in the sentence does this toward every word, all at once.
This is called **self-attention** —
"self" because the words of one and the same sentence attend to each other.

Set this against the two walls from last chapter.

- Wall 1 (distant words get forgotten): attention connects any two words directly, however far apart. Distance never enters the calculation, so the forgetting problem vanishes.
- Wall 2 (single-file processing): the score-checks for all words can be computed simultaneously. Nothing waits for the previous word, and everything parallelizes.

One idea knocked down both walls at once.
That single fact is why attention is called a breakthrough.

## More viewpoints: multi-head

There is rarely just one way to pay attention, though.
Sometimes the grammatical link (who did what to whom) matters; sometimes closeness of meaning does.
One round of matching captures only one kind of relation.

So the same computation is run in several parallel sets, each with its own viewpoint, and the results are merged at the end.
This is **multi-head attention**; each viewpoint is one head.
One head can specialize in grammar, another in meaning — together they capture word-to-word relations from many sides.
GPT, Llama, Claude, Gemini, Mistral, Qwen — all of them have this self-attention plus multi-head arrangement at their core (head counts and fine details vary by model).

## The one-line formula in the textbooks

Books and papers write everything above as a single line.
Its meaning has been fully unpacked already, so a look is all it takes.

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
$$

$Q$, $K$, and $V$ are the queries, keys, and values, stacked up for all the words.
$Q K^\top$ is "the query-key scores", $\mathrm{softmax}$ is "conversion into shares summing to 100%", and multiplying by $V$ at the end is "blend the contents by those shares".
The division by $\sqrt{d_k}$ tames the swing of the scores ($d_k$ is how many numbers a key holds; the more numbers, the more terms in the sum, and the wilder the scores would swing).

We now have a way of reading that sees far directly and computes everything at once.
Still, attention by itself is only a part.
