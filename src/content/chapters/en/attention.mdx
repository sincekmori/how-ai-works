In the last chapter we saw where the old way got stuck.
Cram everything into one tiny memo and hand it down the line like a bucket brigade — so distant words get forgotten, and you can only process one at a time, which is slow.
And we finished with a wish: "**If only the word I'm looking at could peek directly at whatever other words matter — near or far — and do it for every word at the same time.**"

The thing that turns that wish straight into a mechanism is the star of this chapter: **attention**.
As the name says, it's a way of deciding "which words to pay attention to, and how much."
This later becomes the heart of the Transformer, and it's common to every major LLM today.

## One single idea: read with "weights"

Suppose you're reading a sentence like this —

"The dog threw the ball, so **it** chased it."

You instantly know what "it" refers to, right?
The **ball**.
As you read, your eyes naturally jumped back to "ball" from earlier.
In that moment, your mind isn't looking at every word equally — it's **paying strong attention to the words that are strongly related**.

Attention makes a machine do exactly this: **read the strongly related parts more strongly**.
And it does so not just for one word, but for **every word in the sentence at the same time**, computing where each one should look.

## Three roles: query, key, value

So how does a machine decide what's "strongly related" versus "weakly related"?
Here, we give every word **three roles**.

<QueryKeyValue lang="en" />

It's easiest to picture looking for a book in a library.
The topic you're searching for is the **query** (your question), the label on each book's spine is the **key** (its headline), and what's inside the book is the **value** (its contents).
You compare your topic (query) against the labels (keys) of all the books on the shelf, and the better a book matches, the more carefully you read its contents (value).
Attention does exactly this, but between words.

## Give scores, then turn them into percentages

How do we measure "how well" a query and a key match?
Here, recall the **dot product** from the closeness-of-meaning chapter.
The dot product was a calculation that captures, in a single number, **how alike the directions of two lists of numbers (vectors) are — that is, how well they agree**.
So if we take the dot product of a query and a key, its size becomes the "match score" directly.
The better they agree, the bigger the score; if they're unrelated, the score is small.

Once we have the scores, we turn them into **percentages (how much attention to pay)**.
Say the result of matching the query for "it" against every word's key came out like this:

<AttentionScores lang="en" />

The reason we don't use the raw scores is that we want **a split that adds up to 100%**.
This conversion is called **softmax**.
Softmax doesn't just hand "a bigger share to whatever scored higher" — it also **exaggerates the gaps**, making the best match clearly stand out.
This is how a split like "pay 84% attention to ball, 11% to dog, 4% to threw" gets decided.

Finally, using these percentages, we **blend together each word's value (its contents)**.
84% of the ball's contents, 11% of the dog's contents, and so on.
The "**information mixed, weighted by relevance**" that comes out becomes that word's new contents.
That's one round of attention.

## Self-attention: everyone looks at everyone, all at once

What we just saw was the viewpoint of one word.
The amazing thing about attention is that **every word in the sentence does this toward every word, all at once**.
This is called **self-attention** (it's "self" because the words direct attention among themselves — the words of the same sentence).

Now recall the two walls from the previous chapter.

- **Wall 1 (forgetting distant words)** → Attention links any two words **directly**, no matter how far apart they are. Distance doesn't matter. The forgetting problem disappears.
- **Wall 2 (can only process in line, so it's slow)** → All the word matchups can be computed **at the same time**. There's no need to wait for the previous word's processing to finish. It gets dramatically faster.

**One idea broke two walls at once** — that's why attention is called a breakthrough.

## Adding viewpoints: multi-head

That said, there's more than one way to pay attention.
Sometimes you want to see "who relates to whom — the grammatical links," and sometimes you want to see "closeness in meaning."
A single matchup can only capture one kind of relationship.

So we **run the same thing as several sets in parallel, each from a different viewpoint**, and combine the results at the end.
This is called **multi-head attention** (each individual viewpoint is a "head").
With one head handling grammar, another handling meaning, and so on, the relationships among words can be captured from many angles.
GPT, Llama, Claude, Gemini, Mistral, Qwen — every major LLM today has this self-attention plus multi-head at its heart (the number of heads and the finer tricks differ from model to model).

## The grown-up notation (no need to memorize it)

Everything so far is written in books and online as a single line of math.
You already understand all the meaning, so just glancing at it is enough.

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
$$

$Q$ is the query, $K$ the key, and $V$ the value, each stacked together for all the words. $Q K^\top$ is "the score between query and key (how well they agree)," $\mathrm{softmax}$ is "the percentages that add to 100%," and multiplying by $V$ at the end is the "blend the contents by those percentages" step.
The $\sqrt{d_k}$ we divide by is an adjustment that gently keeps the scores from getting too big — when the vectors are long (lots of numbers), scores tend to blow up and make the percentages extreme ($d_k$ is the length of a key).

## Summary

- Attention = a mechanism that **reads weighted by relevance**. Each word looks directly and all at once at the partners it relates to.
- Every word holds three roles: **query (question), key (headline), value (contents)**.
- Score the match with the **dot product** of query and key, turn it into percentages adding to 100% with **softmax**, and **blend the values** by those percentages.
- **Self-attention** does this for every word against every word at the same time, breaking both of the previous chapter's walls (forgetting, slowness) in one go.
- **Multi-head** runs several viewpoints in parallel to capture relationships from many angles. This is the heart of the modern LLM.
