The making of an LLM is now connected end to end.
So what do you do when you want one smarter?
This question has a strikingly clear answer.
Make the model bigger, the data larger, the compute greater.
Performance then improves — and improves in a predictable shape.
This regularity is called the **scaling laws**.

## Smartness you can predict

In a field full of "you won't know until you try", the worth of the scaling laws is that the result can be estimated before trying.

<ScalingCurve lang="en" />

Scale things up and the loss (the "size of surprise" from the pretraining chapter) goes down.
And the way it goes down draws a fixed pattern:
steep at first, then less and less effective.
The shape is so regular that extending the line on the graph tells you, in advance, "at this scale, expect roughly this loss."

Why this matters: training a giant model costs hundreds of millions of dollars and vast amounts of electricity.
Building one on a guess is out of the question.
A rule that says "with this budget, expect this much smartness" beforehand serves as the compass.

## Three levers — pulled together

There are three main levers for smartness.

<ThreeIngredients lang="en" />

Model size (the number of weights — the "parameter count"), data volume (the amount of study material), and compute (time and electricity).
The trick is not to max out any single one but to pull all three together, in balance.
A huge model with too little to read is a big brain given only a few pages of textbook — its capacity goes to waste.
Mountains of material with a tiny model, and the container is too small to hold what's learned.

Try that balance in the demo below.
Move the compute budget and how it is spent (leaning toward data or toward model size), and watch the predicted loss change.

<ScalingBalance client:visible lang="en" />

The bigger the budget, the lower the reachable floor (though with diminishing returns).
And the split should bottom out somewhere near the middle.
On a fixed budget, growing model and data in roughly equal proportion wins.
This idea became widely known through the research called Chinchilla.

## Suddenly able? Emergence

As scale grows, tasks that small models completely fail at are sometimes reported to become solvable rather suddenly past a certain size (problems that take several steps of careful thinking, for example).
These are called **emergent abilities**.
A caution comes attached, though.
Some researchers argue the "suddenness" is an artifact of how the tasks are scored, and the underlying ability was rising smoothly all along.
Best held without a verdict: scale can change the quality of behavior — that much is worth knowing.

## Scale is not everything

Some sober notes to close.
The scaling laws are empirical regularities, and they have limits.

- Good data is finite. "More data" is easy to say, but high-quality text will eventually run short.
- Lower loss does not equal more useful. Getting better at next-word guessing and being helpful to people are not the same thing.
- Nothing guarantees the line extends forever. New architectures or data can change the very slope.

Scale is a powerful lever — but not the only one.
In fact, the field has been pushing hard from another direction.
Could the same smartness be made to run faster, and cheaper?
