The previous chapter showed that a model is a "window you call across the network."
And what you send to the window is a **request**.
Here's the thing: the request carries more than the question text.
It can also carry **settings that change how the reply comes out**.
On a chat screen, those settings were chosen by whoever built the screen — you never touched them.
Calling from a program, you turn them yourself.
These settings are called **parameters**.

## Why does the same question get different replies?

First, let's recall what the parameters are adjusting.
Have you ever asked the same question twice in chat and gotten a slightly different reply?

That's not a malfunction.
As the inference-and-sampling chapter showed, the model picks each next word by a probability-weighted **lottery**.
Because it's a lottery, the reply can vary a little every time.
And here's the important part:
**how that lottery leans can be specified in the request.**

## temperature: the boldness of the lottery

The most famous parameter is **temperature**.
The mechanism is the one from the inference-and-sampling chapter.
Turn it down and the probability hill sharpens — the safest words get picked, again and again.
Turn it up and the hill flattens — bolder words get their chance.

Calling from a program, you set it **to fit the job**.

- **Near 0**: nearly the same reply every time. For jobs that must not wobble — pulling out a temperature reading, answering in a fixed shape.
- **Higher**: richer wording, unexpected ideas. For slogans and storytelling. Wrong for checking facts.

The job coming up in the next chapters — pulling data out of a reply — calls for the low end, no question.
It has a sibling knob, **top-p** (a safety valve that trims doubtful candidates; details in the inference-and-sampling chapter).

## max_tokens and stop: bounding the reply's length

Next, the length knobs.
**max_tokens** is a **cap**: "the reply may be at most this many tokens."
Tokens are the "parts of a sentence" from the tokenization chapter.

Why would you want a cap?
Two reasons.
One is **money and time** — a model's price and latency grow roughly with the token count.
The other is a **runaway brake** — if it mistakenly writes on and on, the cap stops it.

One caution, though.
When the cap is hit, the reply **stops mid-sentence, with a snip**.
Not "at the nearest tidy break."

There's also a companion called **stop**.
It names a **signal**: "when this text appears, end there."
Set it to a newline, say, and you get one-line replies.

## Summary

- A request carries not just the question but **parameters**. The knobs a chat screen's builder chose for you, a program turns for itself.
- **temperature**: the lottery's boldness. Low for jobs that must not wobble; high for creative work.
- **max_tokens / stop**: a cap on the reply's length, and a cut-off signal. Hitting the cap snips mid-sentence.
