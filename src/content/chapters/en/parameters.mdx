The model is a counter callable over the network, and what you send it is a request.
That request, it turns out, carries more than the question text.
Settings that change how the reply comes out can travel with it.
On a chat screen those settings are chosen by whoever built the screen — you never touched them.
Calling from a program, you turn them yourself.
These settings are called **parameters** (a different sense of the word from the "parameter count" of the neural-networks chapter, which counted the weights).

## What is being adjusted, exactly?

As the probability chapter showed, the model picks each next word by a probability-weighted lottery.
Being a lottery, the reply can vary a little every time.
Now recall the dials from the inference and sampling chapter.
The way that lottery leans can be specified in the request.
That is what parameters are.

## Temperature: the lottery's boldness

The most famous parameter is **temperature**.
It works exactly as [the inference and sampling chapter](../inference-sampling/) showed: lower is safer, higher is bolder.
The knack, when calling from a program, is to pin it to the job.
Near 0, the reply comes out nearly identical every time.
For jobs that must not wobble — pulling out a temperature reading, answering in a fixed shape — this is the setting.
For the "extract information from the reply" work coming in the next chapters, too, the low end is the obvious choice.
There is a sibling dial, top-p (the safety valve that cuts off dubious candidates).

## max_tokens and stop: bounding the reply's length

Next, the length dials.
**max_tokens** is a ceiling: "the reply may be at most this many tokens."
Tokens are the text pieces from the tokenization chapter.

Why have a ceiling at all?
Two reasons.
One is money and time: usage fees and waiting time grow roughly with the token count.
The other is runaway prevention: if the model mistakenly writes on and on, the ceiling stops it.

One caution, though.
When the ceiling is hit, the reply is cut off mid-sentence, abruptly.
Not "at a clean break".

There is one more sibling: **stop**.
It designates a signal — "when this string appears, end there."
Make the newline the signal, and you get exactly one line back.
