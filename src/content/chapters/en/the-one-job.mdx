You type a question on your phone or laptop, and an AI writes back, fluently.
You have almost certainly seen that chat screen.

Look closely, and you'll notice the reply does not appear all at once.
It appears from the front, a little at a time, as if it were being written live.
That is not a loading animation.
Behind the screen, the reply really is being made up on the spot, one word at a time.

The only thing behind that screen is a computer.
And at bottom, the only thing a computer can do is arithmetic on numbers.
How does a machine that can only do arithmetic hold up its end of a conversation?
That single question is what this whole book chases.

## Exactly one job

Let me show you the entrance to the answer right away.
The job of an **LLM (large language model)**, boiled all the way down, is just one thing:

**"Read the text so far, and guess the next word."**

That's it.
Write the guessed word at the end of the text, and "the text so far" grows by one word.
Solve the very same problem for the longer text.
Repeat, and the reply grows from the front, one word at a time.

Press the buttons and watch a reply get built.

In the bottom half of the demo, you saw a table of candidate words and their shares.
That table is everything the model has in its head.
What looked like "an answer" was the result of running "compare the shares, take the top one" six times in a row.

## Why one word at a time?

Why not produce the whole reply in one go, instead of one word at a time?

Imagine a machine that guesses the entire reply in a single shot.
Take the vocabulary to be, roughly, tens of thousands of words.
Then the number of possible 10-word sentences is tens of thousands multiplied by itself ten times over — far more than the number of stars in the universe.
"Pick the one right sentence out of all of these" is simply too big a problem.
"Guess the next word", on the other hand, has tens to hundreds of thousands of candidates: a lot, but few enough to actually compare.

Split a problem that is too big into repeats of the same small problem.
That is one of the oldest tricks in computing, and LLMs stand on top of it.
The reason a reply trickles onto your screen from the front is that this "one word at a time" shows through the glass.
