You've seen a screen like this, haven't you?
You type a question on your phone or laptop, and an AI answers — that chat.

Watch closely and you'll notice something.
The reply doesn't appear all at once: it **shows up from the front, a little at a time, as if it were being written live**.
That is not a loading animation put there to keep you busy.
Behind the screen, the reply really is **being made up on the spot, one word at a time**.

Of course, there is no tiny person living behind the screen.
There is only a computer — and, deep down, the only thing a computer can do is **arithmetic on numbers**.
How can a machine that can only crunch numbers hold up its end of a conversation?
That is the question running through this whole site.

## The big reveal: it has exactly one job

Let's give away the outermost secret right at the start.
The job of an LLM (a "large language model"), boiled all the way down, is just this:

**"Read the text so far, and guess the next word."**

That's it.
Once it guesses a word, it writes it at the end of the text and returns to the very same question: "read the text so far (now one word longer), and guess the next word."
Loop this, and a reply sprouts out, word by word.

## Why one word at a time?

And here is where I'd like you to ask: **why one word at a time?
Why not produce the whole reply in one go?**

The reason is the size of the problem.
Suppose we tried to build a machine that guesses a whole sentence at once.
Say there are roughly tens of thousands of words.
Then the number of possible 10-word sentences is "tens of thousands × tens of thousands × ..." multiplied ten times over — far more than the number of stars in the universe.
"Pick the one right sentence out of all of these" is far too big a problem.
"Guess the next word", on the other hand, has tens of thousands of candidates: a lot, but small enough to actually compare.
**When a problem is too big, split it into repeats of the same small problem.**
That is one of the oldest tricks in computing, and it is the foundation LLMs stand on.

And this "one word at a time" is exactly why the reply trickles onto your screen from the front.
It was never a special effect — the mechanism itself was showing through.
