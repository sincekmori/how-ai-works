Chat with an AI for a while and you'll notice something: ask the same question twice, and the replies come out slightly different.
That is neither a coincidence nor a glitch — it follows necessarily from the mechanism.
And the seed of it was already visible in the first chapter's demo.

## Why keep anything besides the top candidate?

Even right before writing "Mount Fuji", the demo's model still kept 5% of its possibility on "Everest".
If first place is settled, why not throw the rest away?

Because it can't be thrown away — that is the answer.
Nobody can pin down the one word that follows "My favorite food is".
For some people it's curry; for others it's ramen.
Since the next word cannot, even in principle, be pinned down to one choice, the most honest representation is to keep the whole picture: which candidates are how likely.
It is the same idea as a weather forecast that says "70% chance of rain" instead of flatly declaring "sunny tomorrow".

## Why make the shares add up to 1

At this point we add one important promise:
scale all the candidates' shares so that together they add up to exactly 1 — that is, 100%.

The reason is that free-floating scores are unreadable.
Told only "8 points on Mount Fuji", you cannot judge whether that is a bold 8 or a timid 8.
Only when the whole is scaled to 100% can you read it at a glance: "91% on Mount Fuji — it is betting nearly its whole allowance on Mount Fuji."
Loose scores get converted into shares of a lottery.

An allocation that adds up to 1 like this is called a **probability distribution**.
Here it specifically expresses "how likely each word is, given the text so far", so it carries the name **conditional probability**.
An LLM rebuilds this entire allocation table every single time it adds one word.

Inside the model, by the way, the numbers are first computed as free-form scores, and a dedicated final step converts them into "adds up to 1" form.
That converter appears in [the Transformer chapter](../transformer/).

## Spinning the lottery

If the shares are lottery allowances, we should be able to actually draw.
In the demo below, draw the same question's lottery over and over.

The same question does not always produce the same word.
Yet as the draws pile up, the fraction of times each word appears creeps closer and closer to its allotted share.

This resolves the observation we started with.
Chat replies differ from run to run because the model does not always pick first place: a share-weighted lottery sometimes selects a lower-ranked candidate.
Holding its hesitation as shares is precisely what makes this way of choosing possible.
How the lottery's dial is tuned is the business of [the inference and sampling chapter](../inference-sampling/).

## Shares make the size of a miss measurable

Holding hesitation as shares has one more important payoff.
It makes the size of a miss measurable.

Suppose the correct word turned out to be "Mount Fuji".
A model that had said "91% on Mount Fuji" and a model that had put only "3% on Mount Fuji" both technically "had it among the candidates".
But it is obvious that one did far better than the other.
With shares in hand, that difference can be extracted as a number.
This single fact is the key to the next question.

And that question has been waiting since the first chapter.
A freshly built model's allocation table should be nonsense.
