In the vectors chapter we said that "a word becomes a row of numbers (a vector), and the closer two words are in meaning, the closer their numbers are."
But one big question is still hanging in the air — **who decides those numbers?**

We can't just type them in by hand.
There are tens of thousands of different words, and each one needs hundreds of numbers.
Doing that by hand is simply impossible.
So how does a computer ever figure out that "cat and dog are close"?
That's the theme of this chapter.
The row of numbers assigned to a word in this way is called an **embedding**.

## The surrounding words tell you the meaning

The clue is **what kinds of situations (surrounding words) a word shows up in**.
For example, both "cat" and "dog" often appear near words like "food," "pet," "cute," and "vet."
A word like "car," on the other hand, does not show up next to those words.

<ContextClues lang="en" />

In other words, **words that get used surrounded by similar words also have similar meanings**.
This is the idea that "you can know a word by the company it keeps" — a principle called the **distributional hypothesis** ("distributional" because it's about how a word is distributed across contexts).
As the computer reads enormous amounts of text, it uses the **gradient** (the "nudge a little at a time" idea from the previous chapter) to slowly adjust the numbers so that **words appearing in similar contexts end up with closer rows of numbers**.
The rows of numbers built up through all that adjusting are the embeddings.
Nobody told the machine the right answer, yet closeness of meaning rises up out of usage alone — that's the fascinating part.

## What it really is: a giant lookup table

Once training is done, the rows of numbers for every word are stored in one big **embedding table**.
To use a word, you just pull out that word's row.
The **number of rows in the table is the size of the vocabulary** (tens of thousands), and **the length of each row is how many numbers there are per word** (hundreds to thousands).
It's fine to think of an "embedding" as, in the end, the act of looking up a single row from this table.

## Adding and subtracting meaning

As a "reward" for turning words into numbers, something surprising happens: **relationships between meanings can be expressed with addition and subtraction**.
Here is the famous example.

Why does this work?
Each word's row of numbers naturally comes to hold "directions" of meaning, such as "royalty-ness" or "gender."
So if you take the "shift from man to woman" and add it to "king," you land right on "queen."
This is evidence that meaning shows up as a position on a map — and this kind of trick is called an **analogy** (king − man + woman ≈ queen).

## The same word can mean different things by context

There is, however, a situation where a single fixed row of numbers gets you into trouble.
Take the English word "bank": depending on the sentence, it can mean the **side of a river** or a **place that holds money**.
If you lock in one row of numbers per word, you can't tell these apart.

So recent models **rebuild a word's row of numbers on the spot, depending on the surrounding words** (a **contextual embedding** — one that changes with context).
"Sat on the grassy **_ of the river" gives you the riverside meaning; "deposited the check at the _**" gives you the money meaning.
The mechanism that makes this possible is **attention**, which arrives starting in the next chapter.
For now, just remember that "an embedding isn't fixed — it can change with context."

## Summary

- An **embedding** is the "word → row of numbers" that comes out of training. It isn't decided by hand — it's **learned automatically from usage (the surrounding words)**.
- What it really is: a **giant lookup table (embedding table)**, with one row per word.
- Relationships between meanings show up as **addition and subtraction** (king − man + woman ≈ queen, an **analogy**), and they can even **change depending on context** (→ on to attention).
