Who decides the numbers in a word's vector?
Typing them in by hand is out of the question:
there are tens of thousands of words, and each needs hundreds of numbers.
But could a computer really figure out "cat is close to dog" on its own?
The list of numbers a word ends up with through learning is called its **embedding**.
This chapter is about where embeddings come from.

## The surrounding words teach the meaning

The hint lies in the company a word keeps — the words that tend to appear around it.
"Cat" and "dog" both show up near "food", "pet", "cute", and "vet".
"Car" rarely appears next to any of those.

<ContextClues lang="en" />

Words that appear surrounded by similar words have similar meanings.
You can tell what a word means by the company it keeps — that is the whole idea.
While reading mountains of text, the computer endlessly applies the previous chapter's "adjust a little at a time", nudging the numbers so that words appearing in similar contexts end up with similar lists.
The lists that accumulate out of this process are the embeddings.
Nobody supplies a correct answer, yet closeness of meaning rises out of usage alone.
That is the most remarkable part of the mechanism.

## What it really is: a giant lookup table

When learning is done, every word's number list is stored in one big lookup table.
To use a word, you just pull out its row.
The number of rows is the vocabulary size (tens to hundreds of thousands); the length of each row is the numbers-per-word (hundreds to thousands).
For all the grand name, "embedding" a word amounts to looking up one row in this table.

Why does this work?
After learning, directions in the number space come to carry meanings of their own — "royalty-ness", "gender", and so on.
So adding the "man-to-woman shift" to "king" lands you right around "queen".
It is evidence that meaning really is laid out as positions on a map.

## The same word means different things in different contexts

One fixed row per word, however, runs into trouble.
Take the English word "bank": depending on the sentence, it can mean the side of a river or a place that holds money.
A lookup table with one row per word cannot tell these apart.

So recent models rebuild a word's number list on the spot, according to the words around it.
In "we crossed the river to the opposite bank", the list leans toward riversides; in "I deposited money at the bank", toward finance.
The mechanism that pulls this off appears in [the attention chapter](../attention/).
For now, keep just this much: embeddings are not fixed — they can shift with context.

Each individual word can now be turned into numbers, meaning and all.
But a sentence is not a bag of words — it is a sequence.
"Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" are built from the same words and mean utterly different things.
How is a machine supposed to handle order?
