A pretrained base model certainly knows a lot.
Use it as-is, though, and something awkward happens.

The base model's job was to imitate the text of the whole internet.
So ask it "What's the weather tomorrow?" and, instead of answering, it may simply keep writing — "...my little brother asked", say.
Answer questions, stay polite, refuse dangerous requests:
the finishing stage that shapes such an assistant is **fine-tuning**.

The finishing comes in two stages.
Here is the overall picture first.

<ThreeStages lang="en" />

## Step 2: imitating example answers

First, the model is shown many examples of good exchanges.
Humans prepare pairs of an instruction and a good response — "asked this, answer like this" — and the model imitates the way of responding.
The activity itself is the same next-word guessing as the last chapter; only the material changes, from the internet's misc text to exemplary responses.

This is called **supervised fine-tuning** (SFT).
Mix many kinds of instructions into the examples — summarizing, translating, question answering — and the model learns to handle instructions it has never seen.
That trick is **instruction tuning**, and it is the source of the "ask me anything" quality of assistants.

As an analogy: pretraining is reading the world's books alone; imitating example answers is practicing with a teacher's worked examples.

## Step 3: tuning on "which is better?"

Imitation alone already makes the model quite assistant-like.
But one push is still missing.
Writing a perfect model answer, every time, is enormously hard for humans.
The limits of human effort show up directly in the quantity and quality of the examples.

So the approach flips.
Instead of writing good answers from scratch, people are shown two of the model's answers and asked which one is better.
Choosing is far easier than writing, so many judgments can be collected.
From the pile of "this one is better", the model is nudged toward producing the kinds of answers people prefer.
This is **RLHF** — reinforcement learning from human feedback.

Opening the mechanism a little further:
from the collected judgments, a judge (the **reward model**) is first built — a scorer for "people prefer answers like this".
The model is then tuned in the direction that raises that score.
These days a simpler route, **DPO**, is also in wide use: it tunes directly on the judgments without building a judge.
After this stage, the helpfulness, honesty, and safety change noticeably.

## No need to rebuild everything: add-on tuning

One more practical point: an LLM has billions to trillions of weights, and moving all of them for every adjustment is heavy work.
So a popular alternative freezes the original weights and trains only small add-on parts (**LoRA** and friends).
Like swappable attachments, they enable per-purpose tuning with little memory and time.

Through this finishing, the model finally becomes the assistant you talk to every day.
Which raises a question about every reply it writes:
how exactly is each next word being chosen?
