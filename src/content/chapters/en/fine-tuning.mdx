In the last chapter we built a **base model** that's great at continuing text.
It's knowledgeable.
But if you use it as-is, you run into a bit of trouble.

<BaseVsAssistant lang="en" />

The base model's job was to **imitate** text from all over the internet.
So if you ask it "What's the weather tomorrow?", instead of answering it might just **keep writing** — something like "...my little brother asked."
To make it into an **assistant** that answers your question, behaves politely, and refuses dangerous requests, you need one more round of polishing.
That's **fine-tuning** (the finishing adjustment).

This finishing has two main stages.
Let's see the whole picture first.

<ThreeStages lang="en" />

## Step 2: Copy good examples

First, we show it lots of **good examples of how to respond**.
People prepare sets of (instruction, good response) — "when you're asked like this, it's good to answer like that" — and we have the model **imitate that way of responding**.
What it's actually doing is still the same "predict the next word" from the last chapter; only the study material changes, from "random text off the internet" to "good example responses."

This is called **supervised fine-tuning (SFT)**.
And if you mix in **many kinds of instructions** — summarizing, translating, answering questions, and so on — as examples, the model can handle instructions it has never seen before.
That's **instruction tuning**, and it's the source of that "ask me anything" feeling of a real assistant.

To put it as an analogy: pre-training is the stage of "reading every book in the world on your own," and copying examples is the stage of "practicing responses with a teacher's model answers."

## Step 3: Fine-tune with "which is better?"

Copying examples alone already makes it pretty assistant-like.
But it's still missing one last push.
The reason is that **writing a perfect example every single time is extremely hard for humans**.

So we flip the idea around.
Instead of **writing a good answer from scratch**, we **show two answers and have someone pick "which is better?"**
This way people can make far more judgments, far more easily.
We gather lots of these "which is better" choices and fine-tune the model so it **becomes more likely to produce the preferred answer**.
This is **RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback)**.

Below, try being that "person who teaches the preferences" yourself.
Pick the one you think is better.

A little more on how it works.
From the "which is better" choices we collected, we first build a **preference judge (reward model)**.
This is the part that scores "this is the kind of answer people prefer."
Next, we fine-tune the model in the direction that raises that score.
(Recently, a simpler approach called **DPO** — which skips building the judge and tunes directly from the "which is better" choices — is also widely used.)
In this way, helpfulness, honesty, and safety all go up sharply.

## You don't have to rebuild everything: add-on style tuning

By the way, moving **all of the millions of knobs** every time you tune is a lot of work.
So a common method is to keep the original knobs fixed and **only train a small "add-on part"** (such as **LoRA**).
It's like a swappable add-on: with little memory and a short amount of time, you can tune the model for each different use.

## Summary

- A pre-trained **base model** is knowledgeable, but as-is it doesn't act like an assistant (it just keeps writing the question).
- **Copying examples (SFT, instruction tuning)** teaches it the pattern of good responses and the ability to "follow instructions."
- **RLHF**: picking "**which is better?**" is easier than writing a perfect example. We gather those preferences and fine-tune toward being helpful and safe (the simpler **DPO** is also catching on).
- "Add-on style" methods like **LoRA** let you tune for each use at a low cost.
- After this finishing, it finally becomes the kind of **assistant** you're talking to.
