So far a word has become a row of numbers, and a neural network can transform those numbers to get smarter.
But a big piece is still missing: **a sentence isn't one word — it's a whole line of words in a row**.
And that order shapes the meaning.
How is a machine supposed to read this sequence?

This chapter is the "before" story for the big invention in the next chapter, **attention**.
**How was it done in the old days, and where did it get stuck?**
Once you feel this wall, the next chapter's idea clicks as the breakthrough it really was.

## Why does order matter so much?

First, look at how much order can change meaning.

<OrderMatters lang="en" />

The words are exactly the same, yet swapping the order flips who does what — who bites and who gets bitten.
What's worse, **meaning can also link words that are far apart**.
For example:

"The old seaside house where **I** lived as a child, with the pine tree in the garden, is something **I** still think about sometimes."

Who is doing the thinking at the end?
The "I" from way back at the start.
To understand the end of the sentence correctly, you have to **remember a word from far earlier**.
That is the real difficulty in reading a sentence.

## The old way: read one word at a time and carry a "memo"

Older language models read much the way a person reads a book.
**They read left to right, one word at a time, and after each word they rewrite a small memo — a summary of the story so far — and carry it to the next word.**

<RecurrenceChain lang="en" />

This "pass the result forward" approach is called **recurrence**, and the carried memo is called the **hidden state** ("hidden" because it's an internal note you don't see from outside).
Read one word, update the memo, move on — repeat until the end of the sentence.

For a while, this worked well.
But two big walls stood in the way.

## Wall 1: it "forgets" distant words

The memo (the hidden state) has a **fixed size**.
No matter how long the sentence, everything so far has to be crammed into this one box.
So naturally, every time you add a new word, **the traces of the older words fade**.
Think of the game of **telephone** — as a message passes from person to person, the original words get blurry.
The same thing happens here.

Use the buttons below to feel that forgetting.
**You can only read one word at a time** (the next word doesn't appear until you finish the previous one).
The further you read, the more the very first word, "I", fades inside the memo.

<RecurrentReader client:visible lang="en" />

By the time you reach the end, almost nothing of the first "I" is left.
But in the earlier example, understanding the final "think about" needed exactly that "I".
**A link between far-apart words gets broken by distance** — this is called the **long-range dependency problem** ("dependency" because the end depends on something far away).

## Wall 2: it can't go fast (no doing things at once)

The other wall is speed.
In recurrence, **to make the memo for word 2 you first need the memo for word 1**.
Word 3 waits for word 2, and so on — you have no choice but to **process strictly in order, one in a line**.

<SequentialVsParallel lang="en" />

The more words there are, the more the waiting piles up in a straight line.
LLMs learn from a **staggering amount of text** — something like a whole internet's worth — so being able to process it only "one at a time, in line" was a heavy shackle.
The ideal, shown at the bottom of the figure, is to handle **all the words at the same time**.

## People did improve it. But the walls stayed.

Of course, researchers didn't just sit on their hands.
They added switches that decide **what to remember and what to forget** (called **gates**), so important information could be kept longer.
These improved versions, named **LSTM** and **GRU**, did good work for years.

Thanks to the gates, the "forgetting" got noticeably better.
But **the two walls themselves did not go away**.
The memo still flowed along a single track in order, so "can only process in line (slow)" and "still bad at very long-range links" remained at the root.

## What we really wanted: to look directly

Let's sum up the dead end in one line.
**Squeezing everything into one tiny memo and handing it down the line like a bucket brigade** — that single design caused both the forgetting and the slowness.

So what should we do?
Flip the idea around.
Instead of hoarding everything in a memo, **let the word you're looking at peek directly at whatever other words matter — near or far** — and do it for **every word at the same time**.
Then long-range links can be made regardless of distance, and the waiting in line disappears.

That single idea — **paying direct attention to whichever words are relevant** — is the star of the next chapter: **attention**.

## Summary

- A sentence is a **sequence** of words, and **order** changes meaning. Meaning can also link **far-apart words**.
- The old way was **recurrence**: read one word at a time and carry a fixed-size **memo (hidden state)** forward.
- Wall 1 — the **long-range dependency problem**: the memo is small, so distant (earlier) words fade like a game of telephone and get forgotten.
- Wall 2 — **slowness**: you can't start the next word until the previous one is done, so everything runs strictly in line. A heavy shackle when learning from enormous text.
- **LSTM / GRU** (gated improvements) helped, but the roots of both walls remained.
- The key is to stop "hoarding and handing down" and instead **look at the relevant words directly and all at once** — that's the next chapter's **attention**.
