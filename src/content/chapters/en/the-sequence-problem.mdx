Words became lists of numbers, and neural networks transform those numbers and learn.
So far, though, everything has been about one word at a time.
A sentence is many words in a row —
and the order they come in shapes the meaning.
How is a machine supposed to read a sequence?

## How much does order matter?

First, see how much meaning rides on order alone.

<OrderMatters lang="en" />

With exactly the same words, changing the order swaps who bit and who got bitten.

What makes things harder still is that links of meaning stretch between words that are far apart.

"**My grandfather**, who built the old seaside house where we spent every summer of my childhood, **still swims** in the sea every morning."

Who is it that still swims?
"My grandfather", from all the way back at the start of the sentence.
To understand the end of the sentence, the machine must still be holding a word from far earlier.
Keep the order, and don't lose the distant links.
That is the job a sentence sets for a machine.

## The old way: read one word at a time, carry a memo

Older language models read the way a person reads a book.
Go through the sentence one word at a time from the left, and after each word, rewrite a small memo — "the story so far" — to carry into the next word.

<RecurrenceChain lang="en" />

This carry-the-result-forward scheme is called **recurrence**.
The memo being carried is called the **hidden state** ("hidden" because it is an internal note, invisible from outside).
Read a word, update the memo, move on.
Repeat until the end of the sentence.

For a while, this worked.
Then two walls rose up.

## Wall 1: distant words get forgotten

The memo — the hidden state — has a fixed size.
However long the sentence, everything so far must be crammed into this one box.
Each newly added word blurs the traces of the older ones.
It is the game of telephone: as the message passes along, the original words fade.

Try that forgetting yourself in the demo below.
It only lets you read one word at a time.
The further you read, the more the very first word, "I", fades inside the memo.

<RecurrentReader client:visible lang="en" />

By the time you reach the end, almost nothing of that first "I" remains.
The example sentence earlier would fare no better: by its end, "my grandfather" would be all but gone from the memo — and yet understanding the final "still swims" needs exactly that word.
A link between far-apart words gets severed by distance.
This is called the **long-range dependency problem** — the end of the sentence depends on something far away, and that dependency cannot be trusted to survive.

## Wall 2: it cannot go fast

The other wall is speed.
Under recurrence, the memo for word 2 cannot be made until the memo for word 1 exists.
Word 3 waits for word 2, and so on.
There is no choice but to process strictly in single file.

<SequentialVsParallel lang="en" />

The more words, the higher the waiting time stacks, in a straight line.
And an LLM learns from something like an internet's worth of text.
Being able to process that only in single file was an enormous drag.
The ideal is the bottom of the figure: handling every word at the same time.

## Improvements came — the walls stayed

Researchers did not just sit on their hands.
They fitted the memo with switches that choose what to remember and what to forget (called gates), so important information could be held longer.
The improved versions — LSTM and GRU by name — served for many years.

Thanks to the gates, the forgetting got considerably better.
But the walls themselves did not fall.
The memo still flowed down a single road, in order — so "single file only" remained, and truly distant links stayed out of reach.

## What was wanted: going and looking directly

Restate the dead end, and it reads like this:
cram everything into one small memo, and pass it along hand to hand like a bucket brigade.
That skeleton itself produced both the forgetting and the slowness.

So flip the idea.
Stop hoarding a memo; let the word currently being read go look directly at whichever words it relates to, near or far.
And let every word do this at the same time.
Then distant links connect regardless of distance, and the waiting line disappears.

This idea — turn directly toward the words that matter — has a name: **attention**.
The next chapter opens it up.
