Attention lets words look directly and simultaneously at the words that matter to them.
By itself, though, it is not a finished machine that reads and writes text.
The blueprint that assembles this part and stacks it many layers deep into one big contraption is the **Transformer**.
Every major LLM today — GPT, Llama, Claude, Gemini, Mistral, Qwen — is built on this blueprint.

## Inside one block: mix sideways, then digest alone

A Transformer is a stack of identical blocks.
Start with what is inside one block.

<TransformerBlock lang="en" />

There are two stages.

1. **Self-attention** (the previous chapter's mechanism). Words mix information sideways, each word pulling in the other words that relate to it.
2. **Feed-forward** — the small neural network from the neural-networks chapter. Each word digests and transforms what it pulled in, on its own.

The two stages exist because the jobs differ.
Self-attention is good at gathering information, but gathered ingredients are not yet a meal.
The cook that works the ingredients into something meaningful is the feed-forward stage.
Gather sideways, digest individually.
That combination is the Transformer's basic move.

## Two tricks that make depth possible: a shortcut and a smoothing

These blocks get stacked dozens of layers deep.
Just stacking them, however, breaks training.
Both the information and the "how to fix it" signal from backpropagation get warped or vanish while passing through so many layers.
Two built-in tricks prevent this.

- **Residual connections**: the value from before each stage is added straight back onto the value after it. This creates a shortcut where the original information and the learning signal travel straight across layers. It is like handing over an edited manuscript with the original attached — nothing important gets lost along the way.
- **Layer normalization**: after each stage, the sizes of the numbers are rescaled into a comfortable range. Left alone, values balloon or shrink until training destabilizes, so each stage re-adjusts the volume to a listenable level.

Thanks to these two, a Transformer can be stacked very deep and still learn.

## Restoring order: position tags

Here hides an easy-to-miss problem.
The heart of attention is a weighted sum — an addition.
Addition gives the same answer no matter the order of what you add (3 + 5 and 5 + 3 are both 8).
Which means attention, as is, cannot see word order.
We just spent a chapter establishing that order decides meaning — this will not do.

So each word's vector gets a tag added to it saying "I am the n-th word".

<PositionTag lang="en" />

This is called **positional encoding**.
There are several schools of tag-making: fixed wave patterns, and tags learned during training.
RoPE, the current favorite, works a little differently: instead of adding a tag, it rotates queries and keys by an amount depending on position at attention's matching step (many current models choose it for its strength on long text).
Whatever the school, the goal is the same: making sure word order reliably reaches the computation.

## Never look at the future: the causal mask

Most text-generating LLMs predict the next word left to right, one at a time (this arrangement is called **decoder-only**).
One rule comes with it.
While processing a word, the model must not look at the words after it — the future.
It is practicing guessing the next word; peeking at the future means peeking at the answer key.

The screen that hides the future is called the **causal mask**.
In the demo below, click a word (or use the Back and Next buttons) to move the "currently processing" position.

<CausalMaskDemo client:visible lang="en" />

From the word being processed, only itself and everything before it are visible.
The future stays hidden.
This rule is what lets the model practice "guess the next word without seeing the answer" honestly.
(For tasks like translation, where reading the whole input first is fair, an encoder that sees everything is combined with a causally masked decoder.)

## What the stack adds up to

Now the whole picture.
Words with position tags pass through block after block — mixing sideways, digesting individually — growing a little richer each time, dozens of layers over.
The pattern from the neural-networks chapter, "shallow layers find simple clues, deep layers find complex ones", plays out here too.
Early blocks catch word links and turns of phrase; deep blocks catch the meaning of the sentence and the drift of the conversation.

Then, the exit.
The vector at the position being predicted, as it leaves the last layer, is scored against every candidate in the vocabulary, and softmax converts that row of scores into shares that add up to 1.
What comes out is the allocation table from the very first chapter's demo.
The "converter that turns scores into shares at the end", promised in the probability chapter, is this softmax at the exit.

The blueprint of the guess-the-next-word machine is now connected, entrance to exit.
What remains is how a freshly built one actually gets trained.
