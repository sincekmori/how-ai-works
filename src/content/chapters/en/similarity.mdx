In the previous chapter, "cat" and "dog" both became lists of numbers — vectors.
What remains is to get "cat is close to dog" out of a calculation.
In other words, we need a ruler for closeness of meaning.

## First try: ordinary distance

When you hear "closeness", what comes to mind is straight-line distance.
Two points on a map, a ruler laid between them — that length.
Draw vectors as points, and word-to-word distance can be measured the same way.
So that everything fits on paper, let's use words with just two aspects: movement-likeness and speed.

<DistanceTriangle lang="en" />

You don't need an actual ruler; distance comes out of a calculation.
As the figure shows, a right triangle hides between any two points, with the horizontal gap and the vertical gap as its two legs — and the distance is the slanted side.
The slanted side of a right triangle is given by the Pythagorean theorem.

**distance = √(horizontal gap² + vertical gap²)**

For two points 3 apart across and 4 apart up: distance = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5.
For "run" (0.6, 0.6) and "walk" (0.7, 0.15) in the figure, the gaps are 0.1 across and 0.45 up, so distance = √(0.1² + 0.45²) ≈ 0.46.
Three aspects, a hundred aspects — the same formula works; you just add more terms.
This is what we ordinarily call "distance".

## Words that differ only in intensity

Distance is a perfectly respectable ruler.
But turn it on word meanings, and something odd happens.

<LengthVsDirection lang="en" />

Some pairs of words have nearly the same content and differ only in intensity.
Take "run" and "sprint".
Sprint is a stronger version of run.
The balance between its aspects stays the same, while every number grows in step.
In the arrow picture: the same direction, twice the length.

Measure the distances.
Run and sprint: gaps of 0.6 across and 0.6 up, so distance = √(0.6² + 0.6²) ≈ 0.85.
Run and walk: as computed above, about 0.46.
The verdict comes out as "walk is closer to run than sprint is" — even though sprint is run's closest relative in meaning.

Untangling the cause: a list of numbers mixes two kinds of information.
One is the overall size of the numbers — the arrow's length, the word's intensity.
The other is the balance between aspects — the arrow's direction.
And the content of a meaning lives in the balance, the direction.
Distance counts both kinds of difference at once, so the gap in intensity got booked as a gap in meaning.
If what we want to know is "are the meanings close?", measuring how well the directions line up fits the goal better.

## Measuring alignment with multiplication

How do you turn "how well two directions line up" into a single number?
The tool this time is not subtraction but multiplication.
Multiply the two words' numbers aspect by aspect, then add everything up.

Why multiplication?
Because multiplication has the property that the result is large only when both factors are large.
Both large: large × large = very large.
Only one large: large × small = small.
Opposite signs (one plus, one minus): the result is negative.
So multiplication automatically awards points to aspects that agree and deducts points from aspects that clash.
The total is the alignment score.

Try it with the numbers from the vectors chapter.
With cat = (0.9, 0.2, 0.6) and dog = (0.8, 0.3, 0.7),

0.9 × 0.8 + 0.2 × 0.3 + 0.6 × 0.7 = 0.72 + 0.06 + 0.42 = 1.20

The "animal-likeness" term, 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72, where both are large, lifts the total.
For cat and car (0.1, 0.7, 0.9), that term shrivels to 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.09, and the total comes out small.
Just as we hoped: the closer the meanings, the bigger the score.

This "multiply matching aspects, then add it all up" calculation is called the **dot product**.
It is the single calculation you will meet most often in the rest of this book, so keep the name.

## Finishing touch: cancelling intensity

The score is not quite finished, though.
Multiplication is also affected by intensity.
Two words whose numbers are all large will score high even if their directions are somewhat off.

The fix is simple: before comparing, shrink every arrow to length 1.
Just divide each list of numbers by its own arrow's length.
Equivalently, you can divide the dot product by the product of the two lengths — the result is the same.
And an arrow's length is its distance from the origin, which the Pythagorean theorem already gives us.

Check it with numbers.
Run's length is the distance from the origin to (0.6, 0.6): √(0.6² + 0.6²) ≈ 0.85.
Sprint is twice that, about 1.7, and walk is √(0.7² + 0.15²) ≈ 0.72.
The dot products: run and sprint give 0.6 × 1.2 + 0.6 × 1.2 = 1.44; run and walk give 0.6 × 0.7 + 0.6 × 0.15 = 0.51.
Now divide by the lengths.

1.44 ÷ (0.85 × 1.7) ≈ 1.0

0.51 ÷ (0.85 × 0.72) ≈ 0.84

The moment intensity is cancelled, the scores match the feel of the meanings: run and sprint point exactly the same way (1.0), and walk sits farther off (0.84).
This ruler reads 1 for same direction, 0 for unrelated, and −1 for opposite (it goes by the name **cosine similarity**).
Every number in this chapter's examples was positive, but real word vectors contain negative numbers too, so "opposite" genuinely occurs.

## Distance or direction — which to use?

Neither distance nor direction is "the correct" ruler.
If you want to know "are these exactly the same?", distance fits; if you want "is the balance of meaning similar?", direction fits.
Rulers are things humans get to design to match the goal.
