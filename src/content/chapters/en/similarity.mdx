In the previous chapter, words became rows of numbers (vectors).
"Cat" is a row of numbers, and so is "dog".
So how do we get "cat and dog are close" by calculation?
In this chapter we build that ruler.

## First: the ordinary "distance"

When you hear "closeness", what comes to mind is the straight-line **distance**.
For two spots on a map, it's the length you'd measure with a ruler.
Words can be measured the same way: draw each vector as a point, and measure the distance between the points.
To keep the picture drawable, let's use words with just two sides here (movement and speed).

<DistanceTriangle lang="en" />

You don't need a ruler — distance can be computed.
As the figure shows, hiding between the two points is a right triangle whose two legs are "the difference across" and "the difference up".
The distance is the slanted side, and the slanted side of a right triangle comes from the **Pythagorean theorem**:

**distance = √(difference across² + difference up²)**

Two points that are 3 apart across and 4 apart up: distance = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = **5**.
For "run" (0.6, 0.6) and "walk" (0.7, 0.15) in the figure, the differences are 0.1 across and 0.45 up, so distance = √(0.1² + 0.45²) = √(0.01 + 0.2025) ≈ **0.46**.
With 3 sides, or 100, the same formula works — you just add more terms.
This is what we ordinarily call "distance".

## But there's a momentum trap

Distance is a perfectly sensible, respectable ruler.
But use it to compare word meanings, and something goes wrong.

<LengthVsDirection lang="en" />

Language is full of pairs that mean nearly the same thing at different strengths.
Take "run" and "sprint".
Sprinting is just running, turned up.
So the balance between the sides stays the same while all the numbers grow together.
In the arrow picture: **the same direction, at twice the length**.

Now measure the distances.
Run and sprint: the differences are 0.6 across and 0.6 up, so distance = √(0.6² + 0.6²) ≈ **0.85**.
Run and walk: as computed above, ≈ **0.46**.
So "walk" gets ruled **closer** to "run" than "sprint" is — its closest relative in meaning, judged the more distant one.

Here is the trick behind the puzzle: a row of numbers carries two kinds of information mixed together.
One is the overall size of the numbers.
In the arrow picture that is the **length** — the word's momentum.
The other is the balance between the sides.
In the arrow picture that is the **direction**.
And it is the balance (the direction) that carries the meaning.
Distance counts both kinds of difference at once, so the gap in momentum masqueraded as a gap in meaning.
That's why, for "is the meaning close?", measuring **whether the directions line up** fits the purpose better.

## Measuring how directions line up, with multiplication (the dot product)

So how do we turn "the directions line up" into a single number?
This time the tool is not subtraction but **multiplication**.
Take the numbers for the same side from the two words, multiply them, and add it all up.

Why multiplication?
Because multiplication has the property of "only getting big when both are big".
When both are big: "big × big = very big".
When only one is big: "big × small = small".
When the directions are opposite (one plus, one minus): "minus".
So multiplication automatically hands a **reward to sides that get along, and a penalty to sides that don't**.
Adding it all up gives a "total of how well they get along" — a score for how well the directions line up.

For example, with the table from the vectors chapter, if cat = (0.9, 0.2, 0.6) and dog = (0.8, 0.3, 0.7), then

0.9 × 0.8 + 0.2 × 0.3 + 0.6 × 0.7 = 0.72 + 0.06 + 0.42 = **1.20**

The "animal-ness" that is big in both, 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72, pushes the total way up.
For cat and car (0.1, 0.7, 0.9), on the other hand, animal-ness shrinks to 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.09, so the total comes out small.
Just as we wanted: "the more alike the meaning, the bigger the score".

This "multiply matching sides and add it all up" calculation is called the **dot product**.
It is the one calculation you will meet again most often in this book, so keep the name.

## The finishing touch: cancel the momentum

This score isn't finished yet, though.
Multiplication, too, is swayed by momentum.
Two words with big numbers across the board get a big total even when their directions are somewhat off.

The fix is simple.
**Before comparing, shrink or stretch every arrow to length 1.**
How?
Divide each row of numbers by its own arrow's length.
In the arithmetic, dividing the dot product by "the two lengths multiplied together" gives the same result in one go.
And an arrow's length is its distance from the origin — so the Pythagorean theorem from earlier computes it.

Let's check with actual numbers.
Run's length is its distance from the origin (0.6, 0.6), so once again √(0.6² + 0.6²) ≈ 0.85.
Sprint is twice that, ≈ 1.7.
Walk is √(0.7² + 0.15²) ≈ 0.72.
Dot products: run and sprint give 0.6 × 1.2 + 0.6 × 1.2 = **1.44**; run and walk give 0.6 × 0.7 + 0.6 × 0.15 = **0.51**.
Now divide.

1.44 ÷ (0.85 × 1.7) ≈ **1.0**

0.51 ÷ (0.85 × 0.72) ≈ **0.84**

The moment the momentum is cancelled, the scores match our intuition about meaning: run and sprint point exactly the same way (1.0), and walk is farther off (0.84).
On this ruler, same direction = 1, completely unrelated = 0, and opposite = −1.
(All the numbers in this chapter were positive, but real word vectors go negative too, so "opposite" really does happen.)
This is the ruler for "closeness of meaning" we'll use throughout this book.

## Rulers are designed to fit a purpose

One last perspective.
Neither distance nor direction is the single "correct" ruler.
If you want to know "are they exactly the same?", distance fits; if you want "is the balance of meaning alike?", direction fits.
**Rulers are something humans get to design, to fit the purpose.**

## Summary

- The "closeness" everyone pictures is **distance**. The Pythagorean theorem — √(the squared differences, added up) — computes it for any number of sides.
- But distance also counts the meaning-irrelevant gap in **momentum** (arrow length). The meaning lives in the **direction** (the balance).
- How well directions line up is measured by the **dot product** (multiply matching sides, add it all up). Divide by the lengths to cancel the momentum, and you get a friendly score from 1 (same direction) to −1 (opposite).

The toolbox now holds "vectors" and "a ruler".
