The reading machine is built — but a freshly made Transformer has random weights and knows nothing.
Where do the vast knowledge and command of language come from?
From a stage called **pretraining**:
the study-forever stage.

## The whole job is one game

What happens in pretraining is almost disappointingly simple.
Show the model a text up to some point, and have it guess the next word.
That's all — repeated over a staggering amount of text.

The biggest reason this simple game works is that no human has to prepare the answers.

<SelfLabel lang="en" />

Hide the next word, and the hidden word itself is the correct answer.
The answers are already inside the text.
Learning where the data carries its own answers is called **self-supervised** learning.
Thanks to it, books, news, web pages, and program code — the world's existing text — can be used as training material as-is.
No army of millions labeling answers by hand.

## Why does guessing words make you smart?

It is tempting to doubt that something this simple could produce intelligence.
But try to guess next words well, and all sorts of abilities become necessary.

<SelfSupervisedExamples lang="en" />

To fill in "The tallest mountain in Japan is \_\_\_", you need knowledge.
"3 + 4 = \_\_\_" needs arithmetic; "The past tense of 'go' is \_\_\_" needs grammar; "It started raining, so she opened her \_\_\_" needs common sense.
The harder the model works at guessing the next word, the more it picks up knowledge, arithmetic, grammar, and common sense — as side effects.
That is how one game becomes a universal drill.

## Guess, be surprised, adjust

The mechanism of improvement is exactly the loop from the probability and neural-network chapters.
Predict.
Measure the miss.
Adjust.

Remember that the model never commits to a single next word; it assigns each candidate a probability.
If it gave the correct word a high probability, well done.
If it gave the correct word only a low probability, it was that surprised — the miss was that large.
The size of this surprise is called the **loss**, and the weights get moved a little in whichever direction shrinks it.

Run one such step in the demo below.
The exercise: guess the next word after "The capital of Japan is \_\_\_".
At first the model is unsure, but each press of "learn" raises the probability of the correct "Tokyo" and lowers the loss.

<NextWordTrainer client:visible lang="en" />

This repeats billions of times, over ever-changing text.
Each individual adjustment is tiny, but stacked in astronomical numbers, they let the model swallow the world of language whole.

## How big an undertaking is this?

The defining feature of this study session is its scale.
The material is a gigantic mass of text gathered from books and the web (with careful preparation — selecting quality text, removing duplicates).
It runs for weeks on end across large fleets of high-performance computers (GPUs).
This stage consumes more money and electricity than anything else in LLM building.
"How big a model should you train, on how much data, with how much compute?" turns out to follow laws of its own, covered in [the scaling laws chapter](../scaling-laws/).

There is craft in how the weights get moved, too.
The step size (the **learning rate**) starts small and gets tuned as training goes.
Set off at full stride, and the training itself falls over.

## What comes out of pretraining?

The finished model writes continuations of text with startling fluency.
A model at this stage is called a **base model**.

But what a base model is good at is, strictly, "writing a plausible continuation".
It may answer your question with another question, or go right along with a dangerous request — the manners of a helpful assistant are still unknown to it.
Between "good at continuing text" and "useful as an assistant" there remains a step.
