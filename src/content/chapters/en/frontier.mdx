This chapter surveys the frontier that is actively advancing on the foundation built so far.
None of it belongs to any single company; open and closed models alike are adopting these directions widely.
And all of it can be understood with nothing but the vocabulary of the previous chapters.

## Routing to experts: MoE

Each Transformer block contains the feed-forward stage where every word digests its information.
Passing every word through the whole thing, every time, is expensive.
So instead: prepare many small experts (mini feed-forwards), and let each word use only a few of them.
This arrangement is **MoE** (Mixture of Experts).

<MoERouter client:visible lang="en" />

A dispatcher called the router decides "this word goes to this expert", putting only the top few to work.
With many experts in total, the capacity for knowledge is large.
Yet each word uses only a few, so the computation stays light.
Open many service windows, and assign each visitor to the right clerk.

## Handling long text: long context

"I want it to read a whole book."
Support for such **long context** is another frontier.
The bottleneck is attention.
It builds the all-words × all-words score table, so doubling the text quadruples the table — cost grows with the square of the length.
It is being overcome with tricks in position representation (stretching the RoPE tags from the Transformer chapter) and in how attention is applied (fine-grained up close, coarse far away) — and implementations handling context in the hundreds of thousands to millions of words keep multiplying.
The previous chapter's KV cache, FlashAttention, and GQA are precisely the groundwork that makes long context practical.

## Beyond text: multimodal

Understanding images, audio, and video alongside text is what **multimodal** means.

<Multimodal lang="en" />

Each medium is converted, by its own dedicated encoder, into vector sequences of the same shape a language model already handles — then simply mixed in with the text tokens.
The Transformer machinery you have learned gets reused nearly unchanged.
Translate everything into the same language (vectors) first, then think.

## Thinking in steps: strengthened reasoning

Hard problems are easier to get right by working through them in order than by answering cold.
Same as for humans.

<ReasoningSteps lang="en" />

Have the model write out its intermediate thinking (**chain-of-thought**).
Train that way of thinking itself.
And at answer time, spend extra computation — producing several answers, comparing, verifying.
This family of "take real time to think when answering" techniques is called test-time compute, and it has become a major pillar of recent performance gains.
Alongside the "compute spent on training" from the pretraining chapter, a second lever has appeared: compute spent at the moment of use.

## From how it works to how to use it

With this, the inside of the box has been surveyed end to end.
From turning words into numbers, through attention, the Transformer, training and finishing, the laws of smartness, efficiency, and the frontier.
Even the intimidating latest techniques, once unraveled, are combinations of the same parts this book has shown.

But this book has one more part to it.
Until now, the one reading the model's replies has always been a human.
From here, the viewpoint moves outside the box:
calling this smart machine from a program, making it answer in fixed shapes, handing it tools, and trusting it with multi-step work.
The "how to use it" part begins.
