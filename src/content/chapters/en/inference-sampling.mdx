By now we have a model that has been cleverly tuned.
At last, it's time to use it to write text.
Running a finished, trained model to produce an answer is called **inference**.
So how does a model actually "write" a sentence?

## Text is built one word at a time, then chained together

As we saw in the pretraining chapter, the only thing a model can do is **output the probabilities for the next word**.
Writing text is just doing that over and over.

<AutoregLoop lang="en" />

You feed the text so far into the model, get back the next-word probabilities, pick one word from them, and stick it onto the end.
Then you feed the new sentence back in as input.
This "**keep going by eating your own output**" approach is called **autoregression**.
That's why text grows one word at a time, from left to right.

## The clever part is in the "choosing"

The key question here is "**given the probabilities, how do you pick a single word?**" What the model hands you is a set of **proportions** — like "blue 70%, wide 15%…" — not the answer itself.
And the way you choose says a lot about the result's personality.

- Always pick the **highest-probability** word (**greedy decoding**) → safe and stable. But it comes out the same every time, so it can turn monotonous or get stuck looping the same phrasing.
- Pick **randomly, weighted by probability** → you get variety and it feels natural. But once in a while it picks a strange word too.

So there are dials for tuning how the choice is made.

## temperature: the adventurousness dial

The most important one is **temperature**.
Before choosing, it reshapes the hill of the probability distribution.

<TemperatureEffect lang="en" />

Turn the temperature **down** and the hill becomes sharp, concentrating on the safest word (dependable but monotonous).
Turn it **up** and the hill flattens out, so bolder words also get chosen (varied and creative, but more likely to miss).
For **creative** work like cooking ideas or writing lyrics, go higher; for arithmetic or factual questions, go lower — you pick to fit the task.

## top-k and top-p: safety valves that cut off shady candidates

Even when you raise the temperature to let it be adventurous, you don't want it picking a "clearly wrong" word.
So there are two ways to **narrow the candidates**.

- **top-k**: keep only the **top k** most probable words as candidates (for example, the top 40; everything else is cut).
- **top-p (nucleus sampling)**: add up the probabilities from highest to lowest, and keep only the words you need **until the total reaches p** (say, 90%). Its strength is that the number of candidates changes automatically depending on the situation.

Both are **safety valves** that keep the model from accidentally picking an impossible word.
A common combination is to set the boldness with temperature, then narrow the playing field with top-k / top-p.

These ways of choosing (also called **decoding**) are model-agnostic techniques shared by almost every LLM — GPT, Llama, Claude, Gemini, Mistral, Qwen, and the rest.

## Summary

- **Inference** = using a trained model to write text. It picks the next word one at a time and chains them together (**autoregression**).
- What the model gives you is probabilities. The cleverness is in **how you choose** a single word from them.
- **temperature**: low = safe and monotonous, high = varied and creative (but more likely to miss).
- **top-k / top-p**: safety valves that cut off shady candidates. Set the boldness with temperature; set the playing field with these two.
