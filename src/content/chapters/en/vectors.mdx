In the previous chapter, text became a line of parts called tokens.
But the parts are still made of **letters**.
A computer can only calculate with numbers.
So what number should the word "cat" become?

## Why serial numbers fail

The most naive idea is to give each word a **serial number** (cat = 2398, dog = 2401, ...).
That does technically "turn words into numbers".
But these numbers are useless for guessing the next word: whether two numbers are close has nothing to do with whether the meanings are close.

Fine — then why not renumber the words so that similar meanings get nearby numbers?
It turns out that's impossible too.
Meaning has **many sides** — how animal-like something is, how big, how fast, and so on.
A cat is close to a dog (both are pets) and also close to a tiger (both are felines).
Yet a dog and a tiger aren't that close.
A triangle of relationships like that cannot be laid out along a single number line.

## One number per side of the meaning

So we change our approach.
If meaning has many sides, **use as many numbers as there are sides**.
The first number might be "how animal-like it is", the second "how big it is", and so on — one number per side of the meaning.

<WordAspects lang="en" />

Do this, and "cat" and "dog" end up with rows of numbers that are alike, while "car" is very different.
And look at the triangle that stumped us a moment ago.
On the "animal-ness" scale the tiger matches the cat almost exactly, while on the "size" scale they sit far apart.
The relationship that wouldn't fit on one number line falls out naturally the moment each side of meaning gets its own scale.
In other words, **closeness in meaning shows up as closeness in the rows of numbers**.
That is exactly the property we wanted.
This "ordered row of numbers, one per word" is called a **vector**.

## The arrow and the row of numbers: two faces of the same thing

Here, let's fill in "one more gap" that trips a lot of people up.
If this is your first time, it's enough to think of a vector as "just a list of numbers".
On the other hand, if you learned vectors in school, you were taught "a vector is an arrow (a direction and a length)".
In fact, the arrow and the "row of numbers" are **two faces of the same thing**.
As the figure below shows, the two numbers (170, 60) are the same as "an arrow to the point you reach by going 170 across and 60 up from the origin".
The arrow is the "look", the row of numbers is "its coordinates", and they point to the same thing.

<VectorViews lang="en" />

And here is the most important leap.
Real word vectors are made of **hundreds to thousands** of numbers.
A 1000-dimensional arrow can't be drawn on paper.
But as a "row of numbers", you can add them and compare them in exactly the same way no matter how many dimensions there are.
We **borrow the intuition we got from arrows (close vs. far, directions lining up) as-is, while doing the actual math on the rows of numbers**.
This feel — "calmly handling high dimensions you can't draw, as rows of numbers" — keeps paying off from here on.

## Summary

- Serial numbers "make words numbers" but can't express closeness of meaning — meaning is many-sided and won't fit on one number line.
- So we give a word **one number per side of its meaning**. This row of numbers is a **vector**, and words with close meanings get similar rows.
- A vector has two faces — a row of numbers and an arrow — and even in high dimensions the row of numbers computes just the same.

We now have rows of numbers.
