In the previous chapter, text became a sequence of tokens.
But each token is still made of letters, like "cat".
A computer can only calculate with numbers.
So what number should the word "cat" become?

## Why serial numbers fail

The most naive idea is to give every word a serial number.
Cat is number 2398, dog is number 2401, and so on.
This does turn words into numbers.
But these numbers are useless for the job of guessing the next word.
Whether two numbers are close has nothing to do with whether two meanings are close.

Could we renumber the words so that similar meanings get nearby numbers?
It turns out no renumbering can work.
Meaning has several aspects at once: how animal-like something is, how big, how fast.
A cat is close to a dog (both are pets) and close to a tiger (both are felines).
But a dog and a tiger are not especially close.
This "close, close, yet far" triangle cannot be laid out on a single number line, no matter how you arrange it.

## One number per aspect

So we change the approach.
If meaning has several aspects, use several numbers — one per aspect.
The first number says how animal-like the word is, the second how big, and so on, assigning a whole list of numbers to each word.

<WordAspects lang="en" />

Now "cat" and "dog" get very similar lists, while "car" gets a very different one.
And look at the triangle that stumped us before.
On the "animal-likeness" scale, a tiger is almost identical to a cat — while on the "size" scale, they are far apart.
The relationship that would not fit on one number line falls into place the moment each aspect gets its own ruler.
Closeness of meaning shows up as closeness of number lists.
That is exactly the property we wanted.
This ordered list of numbers, one list per word, is called a **vector**.

## Arrows and number lists are two faces of one thing

Here it is worth connecting this to school math.
If vectors are new to you, "a plain list of numbers" is all you need.
If you learned vectors in school, you were probably taught "a vector is an arrow — direction and length".
That arrow and the number list are two faces of the same thing.
As the figure below shows, the pair of numbers (170, 60) corresponds exactly to "the arrow from the origin to the point 170 across and 60 up".
The arrow is the picture; the number list is its coordinates; they point at one and the same object.

<VectorViews lang="en" />

On top of that comes one big leap.
Real word vectors are made of hundreds to thousands of numbers.
The count of numbers in one vector is called its **dimension** — (170, 60) is 2-dimensional.
A 1000-dimensional arrow cannot be drawn on paper.
Yet as number lists, vectors with any number of dimensions can be added and compared exactly the way 2-dimensional ones can.
We borrow the intuitions the arrow gives us — near, far, pointing the same way — and do the actual computing on the lists.
This habit of calmly handling an undrawable high-dimensional space as a list of numbers keeps paying off from here on.

Words are now number lists we can compute with.
